# Legible commitment and safe exit

**Draft candidate pattern.** The assembled recipe is a hypothesis, not proof of ethical compliance or wellbeing benefit. “Safe exit” means a usable, understood, bounded way to decline or stop without hidden or disproportionate burdens. It does **not** promise zero consequences, eliminate agreed competitive stakes, or authorize clinical claims. No participant or purchasing test was performed.

## Purpose

Help players understand the cost and consequence of a commitment before accepting it, and preserve a usable way to decline, stop and return. The intended outcome is informed, voluntary participation rather than participation caused by misunderstanding or an avoidable exit trap. The pattern can apply to payment, timed progression, repeated sessions or social commitments; it does not assume monetization is present. [C-01]

## Context and prerequisites

The team can describe the commitment's actual rules, cost, uncertainty, duration, stopping consequences and effects on other people. It can change the relevant interface **and**, where necessary, the underlying rule. There is a decision owner who can reject an attractive conversion or retention increase when it conflicts with approved constraints. [C-01, C-02]

Relevant language and access routes must be represented in the planned evaluation. A summary that is technically accurate but unusable by the intended participant does not meet the proposed objective. The team must be able to distinguish player-chosen stakes from a hidden demand. Where serious risk, sensitive populations, payment or personal data are involved, obtain the appropriate specialist review before any real exposure. This pattern supplies neither that review nor a child-facing research protocol. [C-02, C-03]

## Mechanism and evidence

| Proposed link | Basis | Evidence limit |
|---|---|---|
| Accurate, usable information improves the possibility of understanding | Belmont's consent framework distinguishes information and comprehension; the transfer is a proposed product-design analogy | Research consent is not identical to a game purchase or session agreement; this is not a compliance claim [C-03] |
| A usable refusal path reduces dependence on mistaken acceptance | If declining is unavailable or obscure, acceptance cannot by itself show the offer was preferred | Logical constraint, not a measured conversion or harm effect [C-02] |
| Bounded interruption and re-entry can preserve agency across time | Monetization perceptions and dark-pattern reasoning motivate examination beyond the first click | No inspected source validates this exact exit system or long-term wellbeing effect [C-04] |
| Testing the complete commitment can expose failures missed by checking disclosure alone | Asking for a prediction and observing refusal creates a falsifiable check of actual understanding | A laboratory explanation does not establish real-world freedom from pressure [C-05] |
| Preserving legitimate effects on others prevents an exit safeguard from becoming an exploit | A shared activity can impose costs on teammates even when one participant wishes to leave | Proportionality is a contextual design judgment, not a universal numerical rule [C-06] |

The pattern is deliberately weaker than “disclosure makes a mechanic ethical.” Its whole benefit chain remains unvalidated. A person can understand an offer yet experience financial or social pressure; improving legibility alone cannot resolve every such constraint. [C-02, C-04]

## Recipe

1. **Describe the complete commitment.** Record what is received, what is spent or promised, when it occurs, what is uncertain, who else is affected, and what remains after stopping. Distinguish actual rules from marketing language. [C-01]
2. **Map each decision point.** Trace encounter, explanation, acceptance, repetition, refusal, interruption and re-entry. Identify concealed costs, asymmetric effort, loss of prior progress and any dependence on another person's cooperation. Treat these as risk questions, not proof of intent. [C-02, C-04]
3. **Choose what must be understood before acceptance.** Provide an accessible summary and the relevant detail without forcing every user through a wall of text. For variable currency bundles, show the actual additional transaction and residual balance for the selected path rather than a misleading universal conversion. Do not hide real costs to preserve a fictional surprise. [C-01, C-05]
4. **Make refusal and review usable.** Permit a clear non-commitment action and a way to inspect or reconsider the offer. Avoid a flow in which one accidental action completes an irreversible commitment. Extra confirmation is a candidate safeguard, not a requirement for every trivial action; too much friction can itself defeat access. [C-02, C-05]
5. **Design bounded interruption.** Consider saving completed progress, selecting a participation window, opting out of reminders, handing off a shared role or reconnection. Preserve necessary, disclosed consequences to other participants, but do not attach unrelated punishment solely to deter leaving. Test exploitability separately. [C-04, C-06]
6. **Compare with a simpler alternative.** Known-content offers, direct pricing, persistent progression, finite content or no incentive may accomplish the central goal with fewer obligations. Omit the feature when its benefit depends on misunderstanding or a burden the owner will not openly defend. This is a proposed ethical boundary requiring local approval, not a legal finding. [C-04, C-07]
7. **Plan an approved, non-transactional comprehension/choice test.** Ask relevant participants to predict the cost and stopping consequence before choosing. Let them decline. Record misunderstanding, assistance, accidental actions, accessibility failures and objections separately from acceptance rate. Do not expose participants to real financial risk just to make the test “realistic.” [C-03, C-05]
8. **Decide using outcomes and constraints.** Revise when a material misunderstanding or inaccessible refusal remains. A higher acceptance rate does not cancel a serious identified problem. Where uncertainty is unresolved, narrow the promise or omit/defer the mechanism. [C-05, C-07]
9. **Maintain the commitment.** Reopen review when price structure, reward expiry, audience, localization, support or cancellation behavior changes. The explanation and exit behavior must refer to the same ruleset. Do not silently preserve an old consent screen after changing the obligation. [C-07]

### Adjustable variables

Adjust explanation depth, placement, representation of uncertainty, confirmation friction, commitment duration, reminder controls, carry-forward progress, reconnection/transfer rules and re-entry information. The right values depend on the cost and reversibility of the action, language/access needs and effects on others. There is no default timer, spending cap, inactivity interval or allowable harm threshold in this pattern. [C-05, C-06]

**Exit the recipe without recommending deployment** when actual costs cannot be explained, a decision-critical refusal cannot be made usable, the team cannot honor its stopping promise, relevant access conditions are untested, or credible serious harm remains unresolved. Escalate to a domain specialist when payment, child protection, privacy or clinical interpretation raises questions this entry does not cover. This is a stopping boundary, not an assertion that any specific law applies. [C-03, C-07]

## Worked examples

### Illustrative example: optional multi-session progression

A fictional system offers a sequence of activities leading to a previewed reward. The original proposal ties eligibility to consecutive calendar attendance. The alternative retains completed activities, clearly distinguishes a genuinely expiring shared event from persistent progression, and lets a player stop without losing unrelated earned progress. The return screen shows what remains rather than a shaming message.

**Testable prediction:** after reviewing the offer, people can correctly explain what is lost or retained if they stop, and can find the intended refusal/re-entry route without assistance. **Competing explanation:** participation changes because the revised reward is less appealing, not because pressure changed. Keep the reward comparable where feasible; measure understanding and perceived obligation separately from willingness to return.

**Decision branch:** if the new structure removes a valued shared schedule, offer a clearly chosen commitment where feasible rather than assuming every player prefers indefinite flexibility. If misunderstanding persists despite more text, change the rule or representation rather than adding another paragraph. No actual outcomes are asserted. [C-01–C-05]

### Illustrative example: a shared activity with consequences

A player enrolls in an activity where an early departure leaves partners disadvantaged. Before enrollment, show the expected commitment and the actual consequences of leaving. Candidate safeguards include a planned handoff, a reconnection interval or a substitute role, depending on what the game can genuinely support.

Do not promise an unrestricted refund that would enable repeated escape from unfavorable outcomes. Conversely, do not erase unrelated accomplishments as an exit deterrent. A proposed assessment would examine both voluntary stopping and opportunistic repeated leaving, plus ordinary connection failures. Where the game cannot fairly absorb mid-session exit, an honest shorter commitment option may be more appropriate than a misleading “leave anytime” label. All mechanisms are illustrative possibilities, not implemented features. [C-06]

## Tradeoffs and alternatives

Legibility can consume screen space and attention; confirmation can create friction for experienced or assistive-input users. A simple, reversible action may need less ceremony than an irreversible payment. Giving a genuine choice can reduce acceptance or repeat participation. The evidence here does not promise preserved revenue or improved long-term wellbeing. [C-04, C-05]

Changing an exit rule can also create balancing, support and anti-abuse work. When the only workable exit requires a complex recovery system, a shorter or optional commitment may be preferable. Where suspense depends on concealing fictional outcomes, disclose the boundary of the experience without explaining every in-fiction event; financial, privacy and actual progress-loss consequences should not be hidden as plot devices. This distinction requires review, not mechanical application. [C-02, C-06, C-07]

## Failure cases

The pattern fails when disclosure is unreadable, a clearly labeled decline button does not actually decline, an exit saves progress but destroys a teammate's opportunity, or the team measures acceptance while ignoring comprehension. It can also fail through paternalism: removing a challenge or commitment that informed participants explicitly value. [C-02, C-05, C-06]

Observable warning signs include inconsistent explanations, repeated questions about cost, assisted refusals, mistaken irreversible actions, unexpected losses on return, or strong subgroup disagreement concealed by an average. These trigger further diagnosis; they are not diagnostic thresholds for a medical condition or proof of malicious intent. [C-05]

## Counterexamples and do not use conditions

Do not use a successful mock-flow test to certify the entire monetization system, legal compliance, wellbeing, or freedom from pressure. Do not treat a chosen competitive loss as a violation merely because it is unpleasant. Do not offer an exit the production or network design cannot honor. Do not recruit vulnerable participants into real harmful exposure to test whether they resist it. The ethical decision may be omission, not a more polished acceptance screen. [C-02–C-07]

## Playtest

**Proposed / NOT PERFORMED.** Compare two non-transactional representations of the same commitment with approved participants covering relevant language, access and prior-experience cases. Counterbalance exposure when learning permits; otherwise use a between-person design with an appropriate sample justification. Avoid moderator cues, real purchases and pressure to continue.

Before choice, ask for an explanation of actual cost, uncertainty, stopping consequences and effects on others. Observe whether refusal works and whether a simulated interruption leads to the predicted return state. Record confidence separately: confidently wrong understanding is not success. Record commercial intention only as a secondary, non-clinical outcome. [C-05]

**Falsification:** reject the legibility mechanism if comprehension does not improve under an adequate comparison; reject the recipe's sufficiency if informed participants still face a material unusable refusal, unintended loss or disproportionate third-party burden. Revise it if added friction creates access failures or destroys a voluntarily valued commitment. Apparent short-term success leaves naturalistic behavior, vulnerable populations and long-term wellbeing unresolved. Any future field follow-up requires new authorization and a design appropriate to those questions. [C-04–C-07]

### C-01

**Claim:** Make the complete commitment, not merely the initial offer, the unit of design review.

**Class:** `inference`. **Confidence:** moderate — Reasoned design framing; benefit unmeasured.

**Evidence and locator:** No external source; explicitly identified original reasoning/proposal.. Purpose, commitment map and worked examples.

**Support / contrary evidence:** Later costs can differ from what a first screen implies. **Applicability:** Time, money, social or data commitments. **Unresolved:** Which later rule contradicts the initial understanding?

### C-02

**Claim:** Understanding and a usable refusal route are distinct conditions; neither acceptance nor disclosure alone establishes voluntary preference.

**Class:** `inference`. **Confidence:** high — Logical insufficiency is clear; specific causes require evidence.

**Evidence and locator:** GDR-SRC-20261010-F07-008. §3 consent/knowledge distinction; refusal argument is original reasoning.

**Support / contrary evidence:** Same accepted action can arise from misunderstanding or constrained alternatives. **Applicability:** Commitment interpretation, not a legal consent test. **Unresolved:** What constraints remain after the player understands the offer?

### C-03

**Claim:** Belmont’s information, comprehension and voluntariness framework can inform safeguards, without conferring product compliance.

**Class:** `inference`. **Confidence:** moderate — Transparent analogy with important applicability limits.

**Evidence and locator:** GDR-SRC-20261010-F07-007. Part C.1; domain transfer explicitly proposed.

**Support / contrary evidence:** Human-research framework is not identical to a commercial interaction. **Applicability:** Designing future approved evaluation, not authorizing it. **Unresolved:** Which additional institutional or specialist requirements apply?

### C-04

**Claim:** Legibility and bounded exit may reduce unwanted participation, but cannot guarantee wellbeing, revenue preservation or absence of pressure.

**Class:** `hypothesis`. **Confidence:** low — Causal chain remains proposed.

**Evidence and locator:** GDR-SRC-20261010-F07-008, GDR-SRC-20261010-F07-009. Conceptual risk definition and reported monetization concerns motivate the recipe.

**Support / contrary evidence:** No inspected empirical test of this complete pattern or its long-term effects. **Applicability:** Context-sensitive incentive alternatives. **Unresolved:** Does the change reduce unwanted burden or merely alter appeal?

### C-05

**Claim:** A non-transactional prediction/refusal/re-entry test can expose specific comprehension and usability failures.

**Class:** `hypothesis`. **Confidence:** moderate — Directly falsifiable, but the proposed protocol has not been run.

**Evidence and locator:** GDR-SRC-20261010-F07-007. Comprehension principle; proposed test mechanics are original.

**Support / contrary evidence:** Mock behavior does not establish real-world agency or longitudinal safety. **Applicability:** Early bounded evaluation with appropriate participants and consent. **Unresolved:** Does the observed understanding transfer to ordinary use?

### C-06

**Claim:** Safe exit must account for effects on others and exploitability; it need not erase all disclosed consequences.

**Class:** `inference`. **Confidence:** moderate — Explicit competing interests, no universal proportionality rule.

**Evidence and locator:** No external source; explicitly identified original reasoning/proposal.. Shared-activity counterexample.

**Support / contrary evidence:** Unrestricted withdrawal can impose external costs; unrelated punishment is a separate risk. **Applicability:** Shared commitments and bounded recovery. **Unresolved:** Which consequence is necessary and what less burdensome alternative exists?

### C-07

**Claim:** Omit, defer or reopen the mechanism when its promise cannot be honored or its unacceptable risks remain unresolved.

**Class:** `hypothesis`. **Confidence:** moderate — Normative rationale explicit; decision costs must be local.

**Evidence and locator:** No external source; explicitly identified original reasoning/proposal.. Exit conditions, alternatives and proposed falsification.

**Support / contrary evidence:** Stopping may protect the promise, but can also remove a valued experience. **Applicability:** Authorized project decisions, not a standing approved studio policy. **Unresolved:** Who can reject a commercially favorable but unacceptable outcome?

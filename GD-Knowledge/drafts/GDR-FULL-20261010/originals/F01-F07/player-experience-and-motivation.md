# Player experience and motivation

**Draft research candidate — not released guidance.** Identity `GDR-CAND-20261010-F01-TOP-01` is temporary for `GDR-FULL-20261010/F01`. This file authorizes no implementation or adoption. Review and approval fields are intentionally null.

## Decision and scope

**Decision:** Which experiences should a design make possible, for whom and under what circumstances, and which rule changes plausibly support them without substituting compulsory activity for voluntary engagement?

The audience is a designer choosing among mechanics, goals, learning demands and social arrangements before approving implementation. This topic covers momentary experience, reasons to participate, growing capability, curiosity, agency and interpretation. It does not supply player personality types, clinical wellbeing advice, monetization policy, a universal motivation hierarchy, or a demographic targeting formula. It does not prescribe interface layout or narrative composition.

**Working distinctions.** An intended experience is a revisable design aim, not a property proven by a feature list. A motive is a reason to participate; an experienced need is not identical to a preferred mechanic. Here, *agency* means a player's perceived and usable capacity to pursue an intention with consequential action; *autonomy* concerns willing, self-endorsed participation. *Mastery* means an expanding capability to understand or perform, not merely an expanding character statistic. These are operational distinctions for this candidate, not a formal standard. [C-01; C-02; C-05; GDR-SRC-20261010-F01-01; GDR-SRC-20261010-F01-03; GDR-SRC-20261010-F01-06]

A useful research question is therefore not “Does customization create agency?” but “Does this audience recognize and endorse these alternatives, understand their consequences, and still feel free to choose another route or stop?” No fixed duration, option count or achievement frequency is prescribed. [C-09; C-10]

## Core explanation and mechanisms

### Separate the desired experience from its proposed cause

MDA offers a practitioner framework connecting designed mechanics, play dynamics and experienced qualities. It is a vocabulary for asking how an intended experience might arise, not a proof that a named mechanic produces a named emotion. [C-01; GDR-SRC-20261010-F01-06]

For application, describe a conditional chain: **a player notices an opportunity → interprets it as relevant → can act → recognizes a consequence → appraises the result**. The links can fail separately. A consequential choice that is unreadable may not support experienced agency; an understandable choice whose consequences conflict with the player's purpose may support strategic reasoning without enjoyment. Conversely, willingly following a constrained sequence can fit an intended experience. This chain is the author's diagnostic proposal, not an experimentally established universal pipeline. [C-09; C-10]

### Needs provide questions, not an ingredient checklist

Use competence, autonomy and relatedness as lenses for perceived effectiveness, volition and connection. Tyack and Mekler's analysis of SDT use cautions against collapsing autonomy into control or independence, and against treating all extrinsic motives as externally controlled. The paper's corpus is evidence about research practice, not a new trial of every motivational construct. [C-02; C-05; GDR-SRC-20261010-F01-03]

In Ryan and colleagues' Study 4, 730 self-selected MMO respondents reported associations between autonomy, competence and relatedness satisfaction and enjoyment/intended future play. This is not evidence that adding multiplayer increases retention. In Peng and colleagues' randomized exergame study of 160 undergraduates, supportive feature bundles increased several short-term self-reported outcomes, while reported effort did not show the same significant main effects. Bundling prevents assigning the result to customization, adaptive difficulty or badges individually. [C-03; C-04; GDR-SRC-20261010-F01-01; GDR-SRC-20261010-F01-02]

**Evidence-to-application bridge:** these findings justify checking whether a particular activity is experienced as effective, self-endorsed or connecting. They do not justify adding one feature per need. Start with the suspected barrier, propose the smallest mechanism change that addresses it, and check the experience rather than counting the feature. For example, a player who understands a choice but considers both outcomes undesirable needs a different intervention from a player who cannot identify either outcome. [C-09; C-10]

### Agency and mastery can diverge

A designer may offer many options while one is compulsory in practice, or give players a powerful character without evidence that they understand the underlying rule. Distinguish **available actions**, **effective alternatives**, **perceived choice**, **chosen purpose**, **current task performance**, and **capability across changed situations**. This decomposition is an inference and proposed evaluation method, not a validated agency score. [C-05; C-11]

An assistance option can be compatible with self-directed mastery when it lets a player pursue the capability they value. Requiring everyone to remove it would redefine the goal as unassisted execution. Decide which capability actually matters before selecting a measure. Instructional prompting, motor accommodations and sensory access are different design variables; they must not be removed as a bundle. Detailed implementation depends on Presentation. [C-10; C-11; C-14]

### Curiosity needs a question a player can care about

Gruber and colleagues found better recall for trivia answers associated with higher participant-rated curiosity in laboratory tasks. The design did not randomize a game's mystery mechanic, and its supplementary recruitment details were not obtained here. No dopamine-based design prescription follows. [C-07; GDR-SRC-20261010-F01-05]

The conditional design hypothesis is narrower: offer a perceivable discrepancy, an interpretable way to investigate it, and a consequence of finding out. Compare this with freely available information. A missing fact is not automatically a compelling question; obscure controls can conceal the very evidence needed to become curious. A player may prefer knowing the answer and enjoying execution. Preserve that alternative when it does not defeat the locally intended experience. [C-12]

### Social play is an arrangement of relationships and costs

Relatedness is not equivalent to simultaneous multiplayer participation. For F01, assess contribution, recognition, consent, dependency and exit costs rather than using party size as a proxy. Proposed alternatives include parallel contribution, turn-taking, optional delegation and solitary participation. Whether any alternative supports the desired connection is a hypothesis requiring local evidence. [C-13]

Ballou and Deterding's 12 interviews include accounts of continuing through need-frustrating play for expected future content, rewards or fan identity. The qualitative model is not a prevalence estimate, but it is a contrary case to treating continued play as proof of a good present experience. [C-06; GDR-SRC-20261010-F01-04]

A team can therefore benefit from asking separately, “Did you value continuing?” and “Did you feel able to leave?” Social commitment can be willingly endorsed; it must not automatically be classified as coercion. However, a system that transfers penalties to teammates when one player stops creates a specific incentive and ethical issue to inspect. [C-13; C-15]

### Interpretation and cultural context remain open variables

Sicart argues that rule-centered accounts need an accompanying account of actual play and the player's cultural presence; he explicitly does not discard procedural analysis. This is theoretical criticism, not a measured country-level difference. [C-08; GDR-SRC-20261010-F01-09]

Use that criticism to test alternative readings of an action. Donating a shared resource might be interpreted as generosity, obligation, waste, status competition or theft from dependents. Those are **illustrative possibilities**, not findings about any population. Ask players how they understand the act and its consequence; do not assign an interpretation from nationality, ethnicity, language or genre label. The inspected motivation samples do not establish worldwide measurement equivalence. Direct cultural-comparison evidence remains a gap. [C-14]

## Design choices and alternatives

| Choice | Conditional rationale and mechanism | Costs, contrary case and simpler alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Clarify a small set of consequential alternatives | Use when players endorse the goal but cannot predict the tradeoff. Clarifying the rule may make existing agency usable. [C-10] | More branching adds content and testing obligations. Prefer fewer meaningful alternatives over cosmetic option count; a willingly linear route is valid. |
| Expose evidence of growing capability | Use when mastery is intended but players cannot connect action and result. Compare their explanation and performance on a changed task. [C-11] | Scoring may encourage the wrong behavior or embarrass learners. Private feedback or unscored practice may serve the same purpose. |
| Offer investigable uncertainty | Use when forming and testing explanations is part of the desired activity. [C-12] | Withholding can become arbitrary friction or spoilers can remove the activity. Direct instruction or transparent rules may be preferable. |
| Offer ways to contribute without compulsory dependency | Use when connection matters but schedules, skill or communication needs differ. [C-13] | More social configurations increase balancing, moderation and state-management work. A smaller optional cooperative mode or no social system may be better. |
| Preserve endorsed difficulty or discomfort | Use only when the intended experience includes that demand and players can understand the commitment. [C-09; C-15] | Low frustration is not the sole target, but continued participation cannot justify harmful pressure. Adjust the task or provide an exit instead of forcing persistence. |

These are design alternatives under test, not approved studio policy. None authorizes spending, recruitment or implementation.

## Application process

1. **Diagnose the experience claim.** Write a sentence naming the audience, situation, desired experience and an unacceptable cost. Replace “engaging” with an answerable description, such as voluntarily investigating an uncertain rule without being pressured to repeat solved work. Collect competing interpretations before declaring a deficit. [C-09; C-14]
2. **Identify the broken link.** Is the barrier access, comprehension, effective action, unattractive consequences, social risk, or a mismatch with the player's purpose? Do not fix unreadable feedback by adding rewards or fix a disliked goal by making its tutorial longer. [C-09; C-10]
3. **Choose a reversible alternative.** Compare a feature change with clarifying an existing rule and with doing less. Specify how the intended effect could occur and what would count against that explanation. Keep design intent separate from engine architecture. [C-09; C-16]
4. **Prepare a bounded prototype specification.** Define the decision situation, available actions, feedback information and meaningful exit. No prototype was built in F01. For a learning barrier, consult [Supported attempt and transfer check](../patterns/supported-attempt-and-transfer-check.md), only when its prerequisites hold. [C-11]
5. **Evaluate experience and consequences separately.** Pair accounts of purpose/agency with behavior and outcomes. Distinguish voluntary continuation, reluctant completion, mastery, curiosity and social connection. No single retention or enjoyment total adjudicates all of them. [C-15]
6. **Revise or remove.** Retain a change only when its locally chosen benefit survives the cost and harm checks. Record whom it did not help and whether the evidence concerns an immediate session, return intention or actual later behavior. [C-15; C-16]

## Examples and counterexamples

### Illustrative worked example: self-directed investigation

A quiet exploration scenario has a sealed chamber and three labeled observations: air movement, a repeating vibration and a damaged conduit. The desired experience is forming an explanation, not guessing an invisible interaction. The player can inspect each observation, test the conduit or leave to pursue another objective. The hypothetical rule is that restoring airflow changes the vibration and releases the seal. [C-12; C-17]

A version that only flashes the chamber's interaction point may produce successful input without the desired investigation. A version that hides every observable relationship may produce aimless searching. Compare a discoverable relationship, a direct explanation and an optional explanation. Ask what the player thought would happen before the test and why they chose to investigate. The design claim fails when the supposed investigative version produces only unexplained trial-and-error, when direct explanation is preferred without loss of the intended experience, or when sensory access—not curiosity—is the actual barrier. This is a proposed comparison, not a reported study. [C-12; C-15; C-17]

### Illustrative worked example: contributing without feeling trapped

A shared restoration activity lets one participant gather materials while another arranges them. A completed contribution remains useful when either leaves. Compare this with a version in which both must remain present to preserve accumulated work. The intended connection is recognizing each other's contributions, not enforcing attendance. [C-13; C-17]

Observe whether the second version changes coordination and enjoyment, or merely makes departure costly. A contrary case is a group explicitly seeking a demanding jointly timed challenge: removing dependency could remove the chosen experience. The design should make that commitment intelligible and distinguish voluntary challenge from unrelated attendance penalties. F01 has not established which arrangement an audience prefers. [C-13; C-15; C-17]

### Strongest counterexample: an endorsed constrained experience

A player deliberately chooses an authored sequence to contemplate a difficult theme and does not want branching goals, mastery grading or social interaction. Adding those features to satisfy a needs checklist may displace the activity they chose. This illustrative counterexample does not refute SDT; it challenges the inference from an abstract construct to a mandatory feature. Empirical work on meaningful negative emotion remains an access gap, so F01 makes no claim about how common this preference is. [C-05; C-08; C-17]

## Failure modes and constraints

**Incentives:** a visible progress reward can become the dominant reason to repeat an otherwise unwanted action; telemetry alone cannot reveal the reason. Audit deliberate low-skill farming, social pressure, irreversible commitments and punishment for stopping. Distinguish intended strategic sacrifice from an exploit before proposing a fix. [C-06; C-13; C-15]

**Access and localization:** successful action with assistance is not evidence of failed motivation. Preserve language comprehension, input needs and sensory access as separate explanatory variables. Test the meaning of “choice,” “help,” “duty” and “success” in context; literal translation of an evaluation item is not evidence of equivalent interpretation. Do not prescribe visual or audio settings from this topic. [C-11; C-14]

**Production:** every additional alternative creates consequences to author, explain and maintain. Prefer an inspectable small decision to an untestable web of supposed freedoms. Cost statements here are structural planning inferences; no staffing estimate, budget or guaranteed saving was measured. [C-16]

## Playtest and validation

**All tests below are proposed; none were performed.** No participants, build, telemetry dataset or recruitment exists for F01.

The primary question is whether a specific rule change improves an intended, voluntarily endorsed experience, rather than merely increasing required activity. Compare the smallest candidate intervention with the existing rule and, where informative, an omission or direct-information alternative. Recruit for the intended audience's prior rule knowledge, access needs, language context and solo/social preferences; do not infer these from a genre label. [C-15]

Use a matched decision situation, capture the player's predicted consequence, observe action and recovery, then ask about purpose, experienced choice, capability, pressure and interpretations. A later return must be freely chosen and measured separately from stated return intention. Counterbalance where learning permits; otherwise assign comparable initial groups and record prior familiarity. Do not casually adapt a published psychological scale and still call it validated. [C-14; C-15]

Before collecting data, the game team must choose a primary experience measure, meaningful local improvement, uncertainty treatment and unacceptable harms. **Reject or narrow** the recommendation when extra continuation coincides with greater unwanted pressure, when the capability measure fails on changed tasks, when the experience improvement depends entirely on one unacknowledged population, or when a cheaper omission works as well. These are falsification criteria for a local project, not universal numerical pass marks. [C-15; C-16]

# Educational game

**Research candidate · version 0.1.0 · coverage open.** The decision is whether, how and for whom a game should support a specified learning objective, and what evidence would justify that claim. Educational intent is not educational efficacy. This candidate is not a curriculum approval, validated assessment, child-research protocol or implementation instruction.

## Definition and boundaries

Use **educational game** here for a game intentionally designed or deliberately selected to support an explicit learning objective beyond merely operating that game. State the subject, intended learners, setting, prerequisites and use after play. The objective may be conceptual, procedural or interpretive; it need not be a fact quiz. Its measurement must fit the claim. A commercial game used by a teacher and a purpose-built learning game can both qualify under this working usage without becoming equivalent interventions. [EDU-C01]

Distinguish the intended learning from the surrounding form. A simulation without meaningful game activity is a neighboring instructional form, not automatically the same genre. A points layer on a lesson does not by itself establish learning-through-play. Ordinary game onboarding can teach a rule successfully without demonstrating an external educational outcome. Conversely, a game without explicit educational intent can be used instructionally, but that particular use requires its own objective and evidence. These are scope distinctions, not a resolved universal definition of games. [EDU-C01]

Keep age, classroom/home use, language, platform, multiplayer and subject as facets. This file does not assume that educational means young children, that solo use excludes instruction, or that engagement and assessment must always happen in the same scene. No country-specific curriculum or legal requirement was verified in G20. [EDU-C01, EDU-C10]

## Custom research instructions

Begin with **what the learner should be able to explain, distinguish or do elsewhere**, not with an entertaining mechanic. Map each target to the game action and to an assessment that cannot be passed through an unintended shortcut. Document the alternative explanation for successful play: memorized sequence, motor proficiency, prior knowledge, teammate answers, trial-and-error feedback or recognizable surface cues. [EDU-C05]

For each original study, extract population, allocation, comparator, task, exposure, facilitator involvement, exclusions, assessment timing, uncertainty and limits. Do not substitute a review abstract for those details. Seek active instructional comparators and unsuccessful games, not just game-versus-no-instruction comparisons. Separate added narrative, practice, feedback, teacher support and interface demands before assigning a mechanism. The strongest misleading comparison is “a successful game must teach better than a lesson.” [EDU-C05; evidence anchors below]

Future coverage should include adult learning, language learning, accessibility-led instruction, collaborative play, sustained classroom deployment and external transfer. Those are open routes, not areas completed by the two study reports inspected here. Require subject expertise for content validity and audience-appropriate research approval before actual testing. [EDU-C10, EDU-C11]

## Evidence anchors and unresolved numerical precision

**Classroom integration study.** Habgood and Ainsworth’s Study 1 assigned 58 children, aged 7 years 1 month–8 years 10 months, at one English school; 51 were analyzed after noncompletion/exclusions. It compared integrated mathematics, end-level quizzes and a no-mathematics game, with teacher reflection in every condition. Total game exposure was 135 minutes; extra play preceded the delayed test. The reported delayed between-condition result favored integration, but manuscript p. 21 prints `p < .14` for the integrated-versus-quiz contrast while describing it as significant. That contrast remains numerically unresolved here; the bound is not silently changed to `.014`. [EDU-C02, EDU-C04; GDR-SRC-20261010-G20-005]

**Contrary laboratory comparisons.** Adams and colleagues’ Experiment 1 randomized 42 college students to Crystal Island or a matched slideshow. Slides favored immediate retention (`d = 1.37`, prose `p < .001`); transfer was not significant at the paper’s .05 criterion (`p = .074`, `d = .57`). Experiment 2 analyzed 171 college students across narrative game, nonnarrative game and slides; 16 game noncompleters were excluded. Slides outperformed the combined game groups on the posttest (`p = .003`, `d = .39`); the game narrative comparison was nonsignificant (`p = .32`). Short exposure, exclusions and bundled instructional differences constrain interpretation. [EDU-C03; GDR-SRC-20261010-G20-006]

These results do **not** establish contradictory universal laws. They concern different learners, subjects, instructional arrangements and outcomes. The useful design consequence is to test a specific activity against a credible alternative, not to prescribe or reject all integrated play, narration or exploration. The external literature inspected in this batch supplies no general minimum dose, universal learning threshold or proof of far transfer. [EDU-C04, EDU-C05]

## Player promises and varied audiences

A learner may be asked to reason, practice fluency, explore a model, interpret consequences or collaborate. Make that invitation explicit enough that enjoyable activity does not conceal assessment stakes. A teacher may need inspectable misconceptions and a manageable lesson; a self-directed learner may need choice and a believable stopping point. These are candidate requirements to elicit, not a single inferred audience profile. [EDU-C06]

Keep success measures separate:

| Construct | What an observation could support | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Game performance | Completion, score or effective action under the tested rules | Understanding of the target concept rather than a shortcut |
| Assessed understanding | A defensible explanation, prediction or solution on target-aligned tasks | Retention or use outside that assessment |
| Retention | Performance after a declared interval, with intervening exposure recorded | Unpracticed persistence when more instruction occurred |
| Near transfer | Use on a specified changed case preserving the intended principle | Broad application in a different setting or domain |
| Far/external transfer | Use in the actual external task or a justified approximation | Universal competence beyond the sampled situations |
| Engagement | Interest, chosen persistence or another stated engagement construct | Learning, wellbeing or approval of compulsory participation |

The distinctions are analytical requirements for evaluating a claim, not a promise that every project needs a large experiment on every outcome. Narrow the educational claim to the outcomes a feasible, appropriate study can actually assess. [EDU-C06; reviewed F01-TOP-03]

## Characteristic loops and mechanisms

A proposed learning loop is encounter a meaningful problem → predict or choose → act → inspect a consequence → explain or revise → use the principle in a changed case. A session may alternate play and reflection; a longer sequence may revisit a concept with different representations or combine concepts in a new task. Do not assume the entire sequence must be inside the software or must occur after every move. [EDU-C07]

| Proposed causal link | Why it might matter | Evidence limit and rival explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Target concept is necessary for a consequential action | The activity may create a reason to use the knowledge rather than bypass it. | The classroom study provides a bounded comparison, not a universal integration effect; content and feedback design still matter. |
| Consequence exposes a misconception | A learner may revise an incorrect model rather than merely receive reward. | Success/failure can be ambiguous; repeated guessing can also improve game score. This link needs diagnostic observation. |
| Explanation or reflection makes the principle available beyond the scene | The learner may connect action to a more general representation. | Teacher involvement was part of the classroom arrangement; this batch does not isolate reflection as the cause. |
| Discriminating changed cases separate copying from rule use | Different answers under preserved principles make a fixed response insufficient. | A small item set can still be solved through another shortcut. Reviewed F01 is a draft recipe, not validation of any new assessment. |
| Later external use establishes the intended benefit | A task aligned with the actual claim tests the promised application rather than a proxy. | No G20 prototype or external-use study occurred. Appropriate access and prior knowledge remain confounds. |

The chain as a whole is a **hypothesis**. Keep simpler demonstrations, guided practice, an ordinary lesson and a non-game activity as serious alternatives. Remove an integration layer when it distorts the concept or consumes attention without supporting the target action. [EDU-C07]

## Practical application process

**First define a claim and its limits.** Write the external objective, prerequisites and acceptable evidence in ordinary language. Specify whether fluency, reasoning, collaboration or something else is central. Identify content that must not be simplified inaccurately. [EDU-C08]

**Then design the comparison before expanding content.** Draft one representative activity and a credible non-game or less elaborate alternative. Match content, feedback and feasible instructional support where possible. Record differences in time, navigation and assessment cues rather than assuming media labels isolate a causal factor. A game can remain artistically valuable even when it is not the best tool for a particular instructional claim. [EDU-C08]

**Map evidence separately from rewards.** List target knowledge, likely misconceptions, observable actions, alternative explanations and the follow-up item that distinguishes them. An adaptive game should not quietly redefine proficiency as “completed after unlimited answer-revealing hints.” Record what assistance was present and what construct is still being assessed. [EDU-C08]

**Review content and access together.** A subject expert checks the model; educators and intended learners inform context; access review identifies construct-irrelevant demands. Preserve ordinary readable state, narration or alternative input while changing answer-leading prompts. When timing is itself the target, define why and test access consequences rather than universally removing or imposing timers. [EDU-C08, EDU-C10]

**Stop or narrow the claim when evidence cannot discriminate.** Do not expand a compelling prototype merely because playtime increased. A local review can choose the lesson, keep the game but reduce the educational promise, or commission a more informative study. None of those decisions has been made for a real game in G20. [EDU-C08]

## Worked example, counterexamples and hybrids

**Illustrative, not implemented: proportional mixture.** An educational gardening activity asks learners to identify mixtures containing one-third blue tokens. The conceptual target is a proportion, not remembering the number three or selecting the largest blue quantity. An initial supported case is 3 blue out of 9 total. The later items include 6 of 18 (yes), 3 of 12 (no), 4 of 12 (yes), and 6 of 12 (no). Positions and irrelevant decorations vary without changing the principle. All quantities are local illustrative settings, not developmental thresholds. [EDU-C12]

Ask for a prediction before the garden animation, then allow a reasoned explanation, a diagram or another appropriate response. A follow-up with a differently represented whole tests a declared near-transfer step; a real practical mixing task would require additional validity and safety review and is not assumed equivalent. Record assistance, arithmetic demands and prior fraction knowledge. A player who correctly identifies a valid mixture but chooses a different aesthetic arrangement has not necessarily failed the prediction question. [EDU-C12]

**Failure case:** the game highlights valid pots, so high planting success demonstrates following the cue. **Another:** every correct item happens to be the largest total, so the changed case still permits a shortcut. **Another:** a lively narrative requires extensive reading and route finding before a simple proportion decision; the game then measures multiple unintended skills. Compare a direct visual lesson rather than defending the game form by default. These are synthetic counterexamples, not claims that named games exhibit these failures. [EDU-C12]

In a **cozy hybrid**, avoid confusing reduced embarrassment with removal of conceptual work; optional assessment and clear stakes may matter more than decorative softness. In an **idle hybrid**, accumulated resources can mark time rather than understanding. Automation may remove the very practice the learning claim depends on; alternatively, it can move the educational target toward forecasting and system reasoning. In cooperative play, shared success is not evidence that every participant learned. Keep individual and group objectives distinct. [EDU-C13; [cozy game](cozy-game.md), [idle/incremental](idle-incremental.md)]

## Accessibility, ethics, localization and production

These are proposed safeguards, not legal or clinical findings. Avoid deriving a diagnosis, ability label or punitive ranking from gameplay logs. Define assessment use and visibility before collecting data; obtain the appropriate approvals for the population and setting. Classroom participation and voluntary entertainment differ in practical freedom to refuse. Minimize collected data to what an approved evaluation actually needs. [EDU-C10]

Localization can change the assessed task: syntax, number representation, reading load, culturally familiar examples and ambiguous diagrams may matter. Review concept equivalence rather than translating surface text alone. An alternative response modality should preserve the target construct, not secretly substitute a different task. No universal reading age, timer, sample size or mastery percentage is provided. [EDU-C10]

Production includes expert content review, plausible distractors, assessment item maintenance, accessible equivalents, facilitator materials and versioned claims. A rule/content update can invalidate an old answer key. Budget the less glamorous work of interpreting errors and retaining correct explanations, not only game polish. A smaller, thoroughly examined activity can be a better candidate than a broad curriculum with untested claims; that remains a local decision, not an efficacy finding. [EDU-C10]

## Research questions and validation

**Proposed, not performed.** Compare an integrated proportion activity with a content-matched direct lesson, and, where resources justify it, a less integrated game variant. Define intended learners and prior knowledge, allocate fairly under an approved protocol, keep necessary accommodations available, and record actual exposure and facilitator interventions. No children or other participants were recruited for G20. [EDU-C11]

Assess the target principle with balanced changed cases and an external representation, using a scoring rubric fixed before examining results. Keep game scores, understanding, engagement and later performance separate. Record noncompletion and reasons; do not silently delete struggling participants. If later retention is claimed, disclose intervening practice rather than calling a refreshed assessment unpracticed retention. [EDU-C11]

The local design owner should set a meaningful outcome difference and feasibility/access constraints before data collection; justify sample and uncertainty from that question, not from a universal sample-size rule. Reject or narrow the claim when gains appear only in the original game, rely on answer cues, exclude the intended access group, or fail to justify added cost compared with simpler instruction. A nonsignificant result is not proof that methods are equal; report the uncertainty and choose the next discriminating question. [EDU-C11]

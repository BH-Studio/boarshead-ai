# Adventure

**Draft research guidance for a broad family, not a synonym for point-and-click.** This file examines exploration, dialogue, inventory/logical puzzles, knowledge progression and authored discovery without prescribing an interface or excluding action. It does not approve the working definition or claim complete genre coverage.

## Definition and boundaries

The working label **adventure** describes experiences in which investigating situations, interpreting information, interacting with characters or objects, and discovering authored possibilities substantially organize progress. The combination varies: not every adventure requires inventory combinations, verbal puzzles, branching dialogue or an absence of time pressure. “Substantially” is an analytical judgment to document, not a numerical cutoff. [C-01]

[Point-and-click adventure](point-and-click-adventure.md) adds characteristic selection and interaction problems; [action adventure](action-adventure.md) adds consequential real-time execution and its interaction with discovery. Text interaction, first-person exploration and dialogue-led forms can raise adventure questions without sharing either interface. Neighboring interactive-fiction, visual-novel, puzzle and detective labels retain their own files and unresolved boundaries; this draft does not subsume their complete guidance. [C-01]

Mobius’s pre-release description of *Outer Wilds* as a narrative adventure inside an astrophysics simulation offers one concrete creator use of a broader label. It is evidence of that self-description, not a proof that every knowledge-led exploration game belongs here. The distinction between **membership**, **design intent** and **player effect** is retained throughout. [C-02; GDR-SRC-20261010-G03-003]

## Custom research instructions

Start with the question the player is trying to answer, not the interface used to answer it. Distinguish four states: **information exists in the world; the player has encountered it; the player interprets it; the game records an enabling flag or action**. These can disagree. A player may infer a password without reading the expected note; a journal may register a clue the player did not understand. The designer must decide which state authorizes progress and why. [C-03]

For each proposed puzzle, record at least one plausible interpretation other than the intended one and explain what evidence could distinguish them. A rejection should follow the world’s intelligible constraints rather than merely the author’s unstated association. When the intended experience is ambiguity rather than deduction, do not pretend there is a unique factual answer. Analyze knowledge fairness as an information-and-interpretation question, not as whether the solution seems obvious after explanation. [C-03; C-07]

Seek diverse evidence routes: creator accounts of clue revision, original studies that actually measure reasoning or help use, and reproducible scenes spanning dialogue, exploration, inventory and knowledge-led progress. A study of target-finding is not automatically a study of deduction; an abstract that mentions puzzles is not inspected results. Note prior genre experience, language, cultural knowledge and access conditions wherever they affect inference. No source quota can replace this contextual judgment. [C-06; C-13]

Challenge the following: an adventure must exclude action; more branching always provides more agency; obscurity makes a puzzle difficult in a desirable way; the author’s intended association is self-evident; a journal should automatically complete deductions; all help ruins discovery; and completion demonstrates understanding. Do not prescribe suspense merely because an audio pilot once used adventure as illustrative context. [C-01; C-03; C-07–C-10]

## Player promises and varied audiences

Candidate promises include curiosity-driven investigation, coherent consequences, interpretive agency and the satisfaction of reaching an understanding. Some players prefer a tightly authored sequence, some prefer exploring several unresolved questions, and some primarily want narrative continuity. These are research distinctions, not measured proportions of the genre audience. An optional hint can support one promise while weakening another if it reveals what the player wanted to discover. [C-07; C-08]

“Fair knowledge gating” is therefore a proposed local contract: relevant evidence is obtainable through supported modes; the required interpretation has a defensible relation to that evidence; the interface permits expressing the answer; and rejection or consequences do not rely on undisclosed conditions. This is not a guarantee that everyone will solve the puzzle, nor a demand that all uncertainty disappear. Difficulty can arise from integrating evidence even when each individual clue is readable. [C-03; C-07]

## Characteristic loops and structures

A useful diagnostic loop is **question → investigation → interpretation → test or commitment → changed understanding**. Progress may consist of new knowledge, a changed world state, a newly available conversation, a used object, or access to another question. Record which of these matters rather than substituting an inventory count for discovery. This loop is an inference offered for analysis, not a definition that every scene must follow. [C-03]

An authored discovery structure can be linear, branching, hub-based or revisitable. The important distinction is between the order of events in the fiction and the order of evidence encountered by the player. A fixed past can support several discovery routes; several dialogue options can still lead to the same required event. Evaluate the promise made, not the number of visible branches. [C-09]

Gilbert’s dependency-chart account offers a way to express prerequisite relations and bottlenecks. That graph is useful for asking what can happen next, but a mechanically reachable node can still require an unsupported inference. Graph validity and clue intelligibility therefore need different checks. [C-04; C-07; GDR-SRC-20261010-G03-002]

### Practical choices and evidence-to-application bridges

| Decision | Why the choice is plausible | Alternative and tradeoff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Let an inferred answer work without collecting every author-expected clue | If the gate concerns knowledge, enforcing evidence order may contradict what the player has genuinely learned. [C-03; C-07] | Require a witness, artifact or authorization when that is actually the gate’s meaning. State the distinction; do not disguise bureaucracy as failed reasoning. |
| Provide several investigable questions | Another thread can offer a productive alternative to repeating a blocked attempt; dependency charts make the alternatives inspectable. [C-04; C-07] | A focused sequence may reduce memory burden and serve a deliberate reveal. More open threads can add confusion rather than agency. |
| Record encountered facts without automatically resolving them | Re-entry support can preserve memory while leaving the intended inference available to the player. [C-09; C-10] | An explicitly assisted mode can offer conclusions, but it changes discovery and must avoid presenting unseen evidence as remembered fact. |
| Distinguish destination guidance from solution revelation | A clue about where a question can be pursued need not answer it; Mobius’s wandering discussion motivates this distinction. [C-05; C-08] | In a discovery game, even naming the destination may be the spoiler. Ask what the player is requesting before revealing it. |
| Support an optional help or alternate progression route | It can address a blockage without redesigning the default puzzle for all participants. [C-06; C-08] | Help can be wrong, too revealing or awkward to request. A simpler explicit mode, recap or removal of an unintelligible puzzle may be preferable. |

No row establishes a universal ranking. The proposed benefit follows a stated mechanism and remains open to falsification.

## Pattern selection and conditional application

The [Recoverable dependency gate](../patterns/recoverable-dependency-gate.md), `GDR-CAND-20261010-G03-PAT-01`, is applicable when authored progression depends on clues, objects, conversations or state that can become unavailable. For adventures, add a **knowledge route** beside each mechanical prerequisite: what could justify the action from the player’s perspective? Recovery does not require removing intended irreversible narrative consequences; it requires distinguishing them from accidental unfinishable states. [C-07]

The [Player-requested state-aware hints](../patterns/player-requested-state-aware-hints.md), `GDR-CAND-20261010-G03-PAT-02`, is applicable when a player can request bounded help on a live question. It should not require knowing the correct target object first. Gilbert’s update account motivates that problem and warns that incomplete old-save state can invalidate contextual help. The complete recipe remains an unvalidated synthesis. [C-08; GDR-SRC-20261010-G03-007]

Use a staged process: identify the blocked question; establish the evidence and permitted answer forms; audit reachable/recoverable states; select the least answer-revealing intervention suitable to the request; then evaluate coherence, discovery and agency as well as progress. “Least revealing” is relative to a player’s request, not an instruction to make assistance frustratingly indirect. A player explicitly requesting the answer should not be forced through unnecessary teasing. [C-07; C-08]

### Hybrid conflicts

An open exploration structure can conflict with a mystery that needs one fact interpreted before another. A fully linear reveal can conflict with a promise that curiosity determines the next destination. Possible resolutions include independent threads that meet at an explicit authorization, evidence that remains meaningful in more than one order, or an honestly signaled authored sequence. These are alternatives to test, not automatically compatible fixes. [C-07; C-09]

A restorative scene and a threatening scene can coexist, but the boundary must be designed. This entry supplies no reason to inject uncertainty or ominous sound into every adventure. Likewise, a demanding action segment may be a deliberate hybrid activity, not evidence that the game has stopped being an adventure. Route to the action-adventure entry when that coupling drives the decision. [C-01; C-09]

## Worked example — illustrative knowledge gate

**The estuary archive.** A player wants to learn which ferry carried a missing surveyor. An arrivals board records routes but has a damaged date column; a maintenance log distinguishes days when the eastern landing was closed; a witness remembers the surveyor carrying wet equipment. These details are invented for analysis and do not describe a shipped game. [C-11]

The initial author solution treats wet equipment as proof of arrival from the western ferry. A plausible player theory is that rain wet the equipment. Without further evidence, the intended conclusion is underdetermined. The design must either add an obtainable discriminator, accept uncertainty in the conclusion, or change the question. Declaring the witness “obvious” does not solve the evidential problem. [C-07; C-11]

In a revised test candidate, a publicly readable notice states which ferry transported survey equipment on the relevant service day, while the maintenance log makes the day inferable. A player who correctly infers the route before speaking to the witness can proceed **if knowledge is the gate**. If the archivist instead requires a signed testimony, the UI and dialogue should identify an authorization requirement, not falsely claim that the player’s inference is wrong. [C-03; C-11]

The journal records the damaged board, closure facts and testimony as encountered, leaving conclusions distinguishable. A help request can concern the day, the route, or the archivist’s authorization. The hint system checks which state actually blocks progress before offering a response. It must not tell a player who already solved the route to search for a nonexistent missing clue. [C-08; C-10; C-11]

**Counterexample:** a surreal, deliberately unreliable story may not support a unique deductive reconstruction. Imposing a single “fair inference” answer could defeat its interpretive purpose. The correct adaptation is to change the gate and its promise, not to pretend ambiguity has been disproven. Similarly, players who want an unassisted mystery may prefer to leave the question unresolved; optional help must preserve that choice. [C-07; C-08; C-11]

## Representative and negative cases

Gilbert’s older essay criticizes arbitrary or unexpectedly unrecoverable puzzle structures, but its later preface also distances him from parts of the original advice. Use the argument to inspect failure and recovery, not as timeless authority against all death, linearity or commitment. [C-12; GDR-SRC-20261010-G03-001]

Mobius’s creator accounts provide two distinct questions within one production context: how clue-led curiosity relates to wandering, and what happens when a narrative prototype abstracts away movement. They are not independent replications and do not establish an optimal information density. [C-02; C-05; GDR-SRC-20261010-G03-003; GDR-SRC-20261010-G03-004]

The official *Return to Monkey Island* overview uses a point-and-click label and advertises context-sensitive interactions, dialogue trees and inventory. This documents one subgenre presentation, not the boundaries of the whole adventure family or measured interface usability. Its role here is to prevent an interface-specific case from becoming the parent definition. [C-01; C-14; GDR-SRC-20261010-G03-008]

### Narrow empirical evidence, not a complete hint-system evaluation

Refai, Bateman and Fleming studied 16 mouse/keyboard-experienced adults in two custom visual-search games, comparing four assistance conditions in a within-participant design with counterbalanced order. Task performance and preferences were not interchangeable, and benefits varied by technique and task. This supports caution about equating assistance with a single outcome; it does **not** test logical inventory puzzles, long-term knowledge retention or the G03 hint recipe. The article’s summary and discussion attach an over-assistance exception to different game contexts; that discrepancy remains unresolved. [C-06; GDR-SRC-20261010-G03-006]

## Accessibility, localization, ethics and production

A clue is not accessible merely because a subtitle or journal entry exists. The evidence’s usable meaning, its relationship to other clues and the player’s ability to express a hypothesis remain separate questions. Offer necessary information through appropriate supported modes; do not label basic access as a spoiler and withhold it to protect a puzzle. Do not assume that equivalent facts yield equivalent emotional or interpretive experiences. [C-10]

For translation, inventory vocabulary, dates, social customs, homophones and conversational implicature can change what an inference supports. Record the **semantic dependency** of a puzzle—what must be understood and why—and validate the localized route rather than preserving an English string mechanically. A cultural association may be an intentional subject of learning, but it needs an evidence route rather than an unannounced prior-knowledge requirement. These are proposed reviews, not claims that all cultures interpret a clue alike. [C-07; C-10]

More supported interpretations, alternate solutions and hint levels add authored states to review. Keep production scope explicit: a concise explanation of a rejected but plausible approach may be feasible where a new branch is not. Do not promise a living world merely because the script contains many responses. Recording help requests can support later research only under appropriately scoped consent and privacy practices; diagnosis inference or monetized obstruction is not a proposed part of this library pattern. [C-08; C-10]

## Research questions and validation

**NOT PERFORMED:** no game observation, prototype, participant recruitment, player test, telemetry analysis or host retrieval test occurred in G03. These are proposed specifications. [C-13]

| Question | Proposed method | Evidence that challenges the recommendation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does the evidence support the intended inference over plausible alternatives? | Ask relevant participants to explain candidate interpretations after the same accessible evidence. Separately record missing information, misunderstood relationships, and interface inability to express an answer. | Coherent alternatives remain indistinguishable, or success mainly requires external knowledge the design did not intend. Revise the gate rather than blaming participants. |
| Does optional help preserve the discovery the player values? | Compare player-requested factual recap, destination guidance and more explicit help, with content-equivalent access accommodations available throughout. Record requested versus revealed scope, unwanted spoilers, progress and perceived control. | Progress improves but requested boundaries are repeatedly exceeded, confidence is misplaced, or desired discovery is lost. Narrow or replace the aid. |
| Is progression assessing knowledge or hidden state? | Present a correct answer obtained through an alternate evidence order in a future authorized prototype; inspect whether a flag requirement rejects it and whether that requirement is communicated. | An undisclosed order check rejects a valid knowledge solution. Either change the gate’s meaning or make the actual authorization requirement explicit. |
| Does an inferred rule generalize? | Use the corrected F01 transfer distinction: new cases, different correct answers, prediction separate from preferred action, and retained accommodations. | Memorized answers succeed while new discriminating cases fail. Completion cannot be reported as transfer. |
| Does a recap help re-entry without solving the question? | Compare recorded facts with an explicitly labeled assisted interpretation; include interruption and revisit contexts. | The “facts” condition contains an unearned conclusion, or its usefulness depends on remembering unavailable dialogue. Revise the recap contract. |

The target audience, languages, puzzle traditions and access needs determine recruitment and acceptable tradeoffs. Time-to-solution alone is not a fairness measure; rapid guessing and slow productive interpretation can invert that signal. Select local criteria before a study, without claiming a universal sample-size or puzzle-duration threshold. [C-07; C-10]

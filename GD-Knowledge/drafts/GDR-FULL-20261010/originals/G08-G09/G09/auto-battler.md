# Auto-battler

**Draft genre guidance; coverage open.** This entry helps separate composition, positioning and resource decisions from automated combat resolution, then choose the information, interaction, session and progression rules around that separation. It does not supply current shop odds, a winning composition, a monetization approval or a universal balance target.

## Definition and boundaries

The working center of an **auto-battler** is consequential selection, arrangement or development of a force before a substantial portion of combat resolves automatically. The player chiefly controls the conditions under which the battle happens rather than issuing every combat action as it occurs. This is a provisional catalog definition, not a requirement that every action be automated or every game use a shared shop, cards, an eight-player lobby or elimination. [C-01]

Automated combat is not enough by itself: an RPG's optional auto-combat toggle may leave its main decisions elsewhere. An idle game may emphasize unattended accumulation rather than repeated, consequential lineup commitments. Conversely, a limited intervention can coexist with an auto-battler structure when most tactical execution remains automated. Record exactly what the player can change before, during and after a fight; do not settle the boundary by marketing label alone. [C-01]

**Single-player is not excluded.** The Last Flame's developer listing identifies single-player play and decisions about resources, map routes, builds and positioning between automated combats. **Asynchronous is different:** Super Auto Pets describes timer-free asynchronous Arena and synchronous Versus; asynchronous opponent lineups still support multiplayer interaction. In the Battlegrounds announcement, “solo” contrasts with Duos and should not be silently reinterpreted as a single-player campaign. [C-02, C-03, C-07; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-018; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-017; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-019]

The source range is concentrated in contemporary digital examples. Analog auto-resolution, older auto-chess lineage, some commercial deterministic systems and broad regional player populations are not established by this batch. Strategy/tactics parent guidance and the active G08 draft bodies were not inspected; no parent definition or source identity is invented. [C-01, C-16]

## Custom research instructions

Reconstruct each case's **preparation phase, combat commitment boundary, automated targeting rules, shop/draft mechanism, information access, opponent interaction, elimination structure and persistence scopes**. Distinguish within-fight state, between-round economy, within-run growth and account-level advantages. A single generic progression label conceals materially different fairness and learning questions. [C-01, C-10, C-13]

For probabilities, inspect dated official rules for rarity selection, eligible units, shared versus personal pools, depletion, exceptions, lock/refresh behavior and any deterministic guarantees. Treat a probability table from an old patch as a historical rule snapshot, not a current calculation. Seek original player studies of scouting and causal understanding, complete deterministic-resolution rules and documented negative cases of dominant composition or uninformative feedback. Related Riot posts are one production lineage, not independent replication. [C-04–C-06, C-10, C-11]

Compare a synchronous elimination contest, an asynchronous opponent model, a solo run, a fixed-lineup research model and a stochastic shop/combat structure. Do not treat a model with unrestricted access to cards as evidence about a paid or scarce draft. Do not infer player decision quality from placement alone, or that “more viable compositions” has been measured merely because many combinations can be listed. [C-02–C-09, C-14]

Ethics research must examine **opportunity access**, not only direct stat purchases: roster unlocks, extra selection slots, rerolls, paid information, time pressure, expiry and recovery offers may affect the available choices. Keep mechanical opportunity, perceived legitimacy, expenditure, harm and legal classification distinct. Any future comprehension study should use non-transactional representations unless separately authorized otherwise. [C-07, C-13, C-15]

## Player promises and varied audiences

Candidate promises are **authorship of a force**, **adaptation to available resources and opponents**, **watching a plan interact with the rules**, and **learning enough to make a different next commitment**. These are hypotheses, not a survey of all players. A player may value relaxed experimentation, competitive ranking, a dramatic combat spectacle, a puzzle-like deterministic composition or a long solo build. Those goals can justify different timers, information levels and loss structures. [C-01, C-11, C-12]

Automation moves rather than removes agency. A game can automate attacks while leaving demanding scouting, memory, economy and positioning decisions under a short deadline. Conversely, pausing or asynchronous play can remove a timing demand without making resource decisions trivial. Evaluate the complete preparation-to-resolution task instead of equating auto-resolution with low skill or universal accessibility. [C-03, C-15]

## Characteristic loops and structures

Use **acquire information and options → choose a composition and resource commitment → arrange the force → observe automated resolution → retain the specified losses/rewards → revise** as a working loop. A solo run can place route and reward choices between battles. A synchronous lobby can couple one player's purchases or elimination to others' opportunities. An asynchronous opponent model can reduce mutual live timing while retaining comparison against other builds. These are distinct structures, not interchangeable difficulty settings. [C-01–C-03, C-10]

### Drafting and shops: probabilities need a ruleset

The inspected TFT patch 13.23 and 14.15 notes document different shop/pool configurations. They establish that these quantities are version-specific; no numerical value from either patch is recommended as a current or universal setting. Fates' retrospective also describes interface and mechanic revisions, not a stable all-version rulebook. [C-04, C-06; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-015; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-016; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-014]

For an **illustrative** two-stage uniform draw, a desired-unit probability would depend on both the chance of the tier and the desired unit's remaining share of the eligible tier. That calculation applies only if the assumed sampling process is actually the rule. Special offers, duplicate restrictions, depletion, other players' purchases, eligibility changes or hidden state can invalidate it. A tier table alone is not a guarantee of finding a particular unit after a particular number of rerolls. [C-10]

The design choice is therefore not simply “show odds.” Decide which distribution is known, which variables change and which uncertainty belongs to the contest. A clear statement that the next offer is uncertain can be more truthful than a precise-looking unit estimate unsupported by the visible state. Offer a feasible budget comparison without promising the unit that would make the preferred build work. [C-10, C-16]

### Scouting and interaction

Scouting can inform positional responses, contested acquisition and possible future matchups, but only when the relevant state is visible and time permits using it. If opponents can reconfigure after inspection, an observation is a dated snapshot rather than a commitment. If the opponent is asynchronous, the player may be responding to a stored lineup rather than a live player's current intention. Explicitly state what is current, what is sampled and what can still change. These are proposed information-contract distinctions, not claims that all games expose the same data. [C-10, C-11]

Economic interaction can be direct through competition for a shared pool, indirect through opponents' builds or absent from an isolated solo shop. A shared pool may create denial decisions, but it can also make an identical-looking offer depend on hidden aggregate state. Do not promise complete counterplay through scouting when the interface does not actually reveal enough to support the action. [C-10]

### Deterministic and stochastic resolution

Separate randomness in **acquisition**, **opponent selection**, **combat events** and **rewarding progression**. A game can use random offers and deterministic combat, or deterministic acquisition with stochastic targeting. “Deterministic given complete state and seed” is not the same as “predictable from what the player knows.” Complexity, simultaneous effects, tie-breaking and withheld information can still make outcomes difficult to anticipate. [C-11]

The Ludus paper's documented fixed-card match procedure provides a narrow model for examining fully specified resolution; its random tournament sampling is a separate research procedure. A deterministic interpretation of a fixed, vanilla-card matchup follows from those described rules, not from observing a current commercial game's executable. The attempted Mechabellum route was only a community lead and does not substantiate an official no-randomness claim. [C-09, C-11; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-021]

Riot's Set 1 retrospective discusses different kinds of randomness and its preference for counters that do not simply nullify a strategy. Treat this as the creator's context-specific heuristic. It motivates asking whether a player can anticipate, influence or recover from an outcome; it does not prove that every opt-in random event is good or every hard counter is bad. [C-05; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-013]

### Causal feedback without invented diagnosis

TFT patch 12.6 documents recap categories for damage dealt, damage taken and healing, with PC controls. That is evidence of a reporting interface, not evidence that aggregate counters identify why a player lost. Fates' UI retrospective is also a production account, not a comprehension experiment. [C-08, C-06; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-020; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-014]

A damage total may be low because a unit had no eligible target, died early, spent time enabling allies, was positioned badly, faced an unfavorable matchup, or encountered random effects. Several explanations can fit one number. A factual sequence can narrow the possibilities, but a recommendation about what would have happened under a different lineup requires additional assumptions. The proposed debrief therefore separates **observed events**, **declared rules**, **conditional inference**, **unobserved causes** and **an adjustable next choice**. [C-12]

### Numerical balance is not strategic or ethical equivalence

Ludus evaluated a research simulator with freely available cards and fixed lineups, leaving drafting/purchasing for future work. In its vanilla-card optimization, a zero-variance solution made every match tie. That computational result is a concrete failure case for treating a single parity metric as sufficient. Its optimistic discussion of enjoyable balance is not a human enjoyment result. [C-09; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-021]

For a live or proposed design, a card's observed success can also depend on who obtains it, when it is available, what else accompanies it and who elects to use it. The proposed investigation should compare feasible states and alternatives, not infer intrinsic strength from a pooled pick/win chart. This is conditional methodological reasoning, not a new analysis of TFT data. [C-14]

## Design choices and conditional application

| Decision | Proposed application and evidence bridge | Alternative, tradeoff and exit |
|---|---|---|
| Preparation time | Choose synchronous deadlines, adjustable solo time or asynchronous preparation according to the promised interaction. The documented existence of different modes supports treating time as a design variable. [C-02, C-03, C-15] | Longer time can increase analysis burden or waiting. Do not make another player's participation contingent on an undisclosed unlimited pause. |
| Acquisition | Specify personal/shared availability, eligibility, repetition and future opportunity costs. Version probability displays with the rules they describe. [C-04, C-10] | A deterministic draft or known choice set may better serve a planning puzzle; uncertainty can support adaptation but is not self-validating. |
| Scouting | Make observation age, reachable information and commitment timing clear. Evaluate whether a feasible response remains. [C-10, C-11] | Excessive omniscient detail can remove bluffing; too much required inspection can become a timer/access barrier. |
| Automated combat | Explain important targeting/priority rules; record hidden or random elements without pretending the player could know them. [C-11, C-12] | A fully solved preview can displace the intended composition decision. For high-uncertainty combat, a broad factual recap may be the honest limit. |
| Elimination and session | State the remaining commitment, spectator/exit choice, reconnect handling and what persists after loss. [C-15, C-16] | A forced long wait after elimination may undermine the offered session; immediate exit can conflict with a team's shared role. Resolve the actual contract. |
| Roster roles | Compare enabling, control, survivability, timing and acquisition constraints, not only expensive-unit damage. [C-05, C-14] | Some late-game replacement is an intentional progression promise. Do not require every initial unit to remain equally strong in every state. |
| Account and paid advantages | Audit additional feasible choices as well as numerical stats; compare equal and unequal opportunity access in non-transactional states. [C-07, C-13] | Cosmetic, convenience, expression and power claims require separate evidence. “No direct stat boost” is not an equity certificate. |

**Application process — proposed.** Define the intended player-controlled decision before choosing the shop or the timer. Map one complete preparation/combat/reward cycle, marking every source of uncertainty and persistence. Verify the actual information that supports each offered choice. Compare a minimal explanation and a more detailed debrief separately from changing combat or acquisition rules. Evaluate representative access and experience profiles; keep account progression constant or make it an explicit factor. Only an authorized owner can accept the resulting tradeoffs. [C-10–C-16]

### Worked example: losing despite a reasonable purchase — illustrative

A fictional solo run offers a defender, a ranged attacker and an expensive reroll. The player knows the current offer and budget, but not the next offer. They buy the defender to protect an existing damage dealer. The next encounter includes an enemy that bypasses the frontline. Losing does not establish that purchasing the defender was irrational under the information available. [C-10–C-12]

A truthful debrief can identify the bypass event if it was visible, the target it reached, the defender's actual contribution and the positional choices available next time. It cannot say “rerolling would have found the correct counter” without a disclosed and relevant counterfactual. The interface might instead invite examination of the bypass rule and a different formation when that enemy is known. If the threat was intentionally hidden, the game can acknowledge the uncertainty rather than invent an avoidable player mistake. [C-12]

A future learning check should include an unfamiliar enemy using the same bypass rule and a case in which the frontline is effective. A changed targeting rule belongs in a separate adaptation test. Record whether players distinguish those cases, not just whether they repeat “always move the carry.” [C-12, C-15, C-16]

### Worked example: optionality can have value — illustrative

A fictional competitive selection screen gives one account a larger set of eligible starting choices than another, with no change to unit statistics. If every old choice remains available at the same cost and the new set adds a better fit for the known situation, the best available choice can improve. The magnitude and realized player benefit depend on the actual distribution, information and selection skill. This reasoning is not an estimated advantage for a named commercial product. [C-13]

The Battlegrounds Season 9 announcement documents hero rerolls through free and paid routes and additional hero-choice access. Its framing of optionality versus direct power is therefore a question for an opportunity audit, not a settled empirical fairness conclusion. Do not infer addiction, deceptive intent or a legal category from that observation. [C-07, C-13; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-019]

## Pattern selection and hybrid conflicts

Use **Visible opportunity-cost comparison**, `GDR-CAND-20261010-F02-PAT-01`, for spending a known amount versus a known feasible alternative—not a guaranteed future shop result. **Fact-preserving cue alternatives**, `GDR-CAND-20261010-P02-PAT-02`, preserves legitimate facts about offers, targeting and outcomes. **Triangulated balance diagnosis**, `GDR-CAND-20261010-F07-PAT-01`, concerns the designer's internal investigation; it is not a reason to display a diagnostic accusation to a player. **Legible commitment and safe exit**, `GDR-CAND-20261010-F07-PAT-02`, can guide session and offer scrutiny while preserving obligations to others. All remain reviewed drafts. [C-16]

The new [Evidence-bounded resolution debrief](../patterns/evidence-bounded-resolution-debrief.md), `GDR-CAND-20261010-G09-PAT-01`, targets the narrower player-facing explanation problem. It is distinct from the internal balance pattern and remains a hypothesis. Omit it when no faithful evidence trace is available or when a simpler factual summary serves the same decision. [C-12]

In an auto-battler/[tower-defense](tower-defense.md) hybrid, a fixed wave forecast may conflict with an opponent's live roster changes. In an auto-battler/[4X](4x.md) hybrid, persistent empire income can intentionally create uneven battles, which must not be advertised as isolated equal-opportunity contests. In a roguelite hybrid, choosing risk for a run can conflict with ranked competitive parity. Resolve the promised contest, information timing and persistence scope explicitly rather than choosing a genre label as authority. [C-01, C-13, C-16]

## Representative cases and negative cases

The source range includes Riot's evolving stochastic competitive design, Team Wood's asynchronous/synchronous modes, Hotloop's solo run, Blizzard's selection-access announcement and a deliberately restricted research simulator. This is purposeful contrasting coverage, not a sample estimating what all auto-battlers do. The original simulator's genre introduction emphasizes online competitive play; the solo documentary case is a reason not to copy that introduction into a universal definition. [C-02–C-09]

The strongest negative case for a detailed debrief is a player who already understands the fight but needs the preparation interval to make the next decision. A compulsory explanation can become an attention tax. The strongest negative case for equating more choices with fairness is an opportunity increase available unequally; the strongest negative case for equal win-rate targets is the study's degenerate tie solution. These address different claims and should not be collapsed into “randomness is bad.” [C-09, C-12, C-13]

## Accessibility, ethics, localization and production

**Proposed access work:** test acquiring an offer's relevant facts, selecting/positioning a unit, confirming the commitment, tracking a consequential event and revising a plan. Avoid assuming fast reading, precise dragging, color discrimination or constant visual tracking. Give accessible focus order and controllable detail; narration of every combat event can itself obscure the important state. Asynchronous play reduces one timing constraint but does not establish complete motor, sensory or cognitive access. [C-15, C-16]

For localization, treat trait names, targeting priorities, rarity, upgrade/merge terminology and probability language as a connected rule vocabulary. Test offer details together with the actual failure or recap message. A translated “random enemy” must not quietly become “nearest enemy”; an unchanged noun can still be misleading after a patch changes its rule. No specific language or access population was tested here. [C-11, C-15]

For retention and purchases, distinguish cosmetic collection, broader option access, time savings and mechanical advantage. Assess expiry, declined offers, re-entry and stopping consequences without taking purchases or playtime as proof of preference or wellbeing. Non-transactional mockups and declared rule comparisons can inform later research without exposing participants to actual financial risk. No clinical, legal or product-safety conclusion is made. [C-13, C-15, C-16]

For production, a trustworthy recap needs versioned event semantics, privacy-aware filtering and maintained labels. A replay across patches may not reproduce the original rules; a modeled replay that changes opponents' behavior is not the same counterfactual. Simulation can find numerical edge cases while omitting drafting, access and human experience. Cost, maintenance and content-combination coverage remain open rather than claimed savings. [C-09–C-12, C-14]

## Research questions and validation

**All proposed, not performed.** Sampling, consent, local practical margins and stopping criteria require separate approval. No universal number of rounds, participants, viable units or “fair” win rate is specified. [C-14, C-15]

| Question | Comparison and participants/context | Measures, confounds and rejection |
|---|---|---|
| Do players understand acquisition uncertainty? | Compare tier-only information, qualified eligible-pool information and a simpler uncertainty explanation in a known prototype ruleset. Include novice/experienced players and relevant reading/access needs. | Record predictions and confidence, budget comprehension and mistaken guarantees. Reject added precision if it implies unavailable knowledge or increases false certainty. |
| Does a recap support an actionable, warranted revision? | Compare counters-only, factual event sequence and sequence plus explicitly conditional suggestions. Hold combat/rules constant and control prior exposure. | Measure supported versus unsupported causal explanations, same-rule transfer, time burden and next choices. Reject if false diagnosis or hidden-information leakage rises even when players feel more certain. |
| Does deterministic resolution improve the intended experience? | Compare specified deterministic and stochastic variants with otherwise matched decisions; keep outcome variance and rule complexity explicit, not hidden in one “randomness” switch. | Separate predictability, surprise, agency and enjoyment. Reject universal claims when a different audience or task values a different uncertainty structure. |
| Does extra selection access change opportunity? | Use non-transactional, controlled selection states with equal versus unequal option access. Counterbalance offered sets and experience; no real spending. | Assess feasible choices, selection quality, comprehension and perceived legitimacy. Do not infer a causal harm or legal classification from option value alone. |
| Does a numerical balance improvement retain meaningful differences? | Inspect simulator edge cases, then seek separately authorized human evaluation of the intended alternatives. Keep draft cost/availability distinct from combat strength. | Include degenerate ties, role collapse and inaccessible choices as guardrails. Reject “balanced” when a metric improves by destroying the intended decisions. |

Preserve the reviewed F01 transfer correction: unfamiliar cases must keep the same underlying rule, with contrasting correct responses. A changed targeting or eligibility rule is a separate adaptation/discrimination task. Necessary access supports remain available rather than being faded as a reward for success. [C-15, C-16]

### Coverage and next discriminating work

Researched: preparation/resolution boundary, solo/asynchronous/synchronous distinctions, dated shop/pool changes, scouting and uncertainty models, recap limits, one original computational balance study, paid-optionality questions and conditional shared guidance. Open: complete current rules, original human causal-feedback studies, authoritative commercial deterministic examples, original lineup-mining editions, persistent-power effects, elimination/reconnect rules, regional and access populations, and F03/F05/F06/G08 dependency review. The next question is **which decision can a truthful recap help the player revise, given the information they were actually permitted to know**—not which single mistake the system should blame. [C-01, C-10–C-16]

# Beat-em-up

**Draft research candidate, not released guidance.** Definitions and application recommendations below are working inferences or hypotheses, not approved studio choices or universal genre requirements. Source-supported descriptions, creator intentions, and empirical results are separated in the claim ledger. No game was played, no recording was analyzed, and no prototype or player test was run for G05. Public game cases are observations of documentation artifacts, not firsthand observations of their executable behavior.

The [G05 review packet](../reviews/full-run-20261010-g05-review-packet.md) records the inspected input snapshot, selected dependency hashes, source relationships, open coverage, and gates. Existing reviewed dependencies remain unreleased and are not repackaged here. A link to a packet dependency record does not pretend that its underlying topic is included in this four-candidate set.

## Definition and boundaries

This entry uses **beat-em-up** for modes organized around repeatedly managing groups of opponents through character actions and positional control, with encounters connected by stage, route, or arena progression. Melee is a characteristic emphasis in the selected cases, not a ban on thrown objects, weapons, or ranged options. The definition is a working inference, not a formal standard or a market-consensus finding. Cooperative play is a facet, not a membership requirement. [C-01]

The useful contrast with [Fighting](fighting.md) is the **organizing decision problem**, not whether punches occur. A duel focuses attention on another combatant's repertoire and adaptation; a crowd encounter additionally requires attention to roles, approach paths, temporary incapacity, resource opportunities, and other actors. A beat-em-up can contain a demanding boss duel without every preceding group becoming a collection of independent fighting-game rounds. Conversely, a fighting title can contain a wave mode. [C-01, C-02]

| Structure | Characteristic decision to investigate | Contested boundary and misleading shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| Belt-scrolling corridor | Position along progression and across the depth plane; facing; access to enemies and pickups; scrolling commitments | Screen alignment is not identical to attack-range overlap. A side-on viewpoint alone does not make a platformer or shooter a beat-em-up. |
| Bounded arena | Use of traversable space, admission of threats, environmental opportunities, exit conditions | An arena is not a genre by itself. Inspect whether group control or another system organizes decisions. |
| Three-dimensional martial-arts/action hybrid | Orientation, targeting, occlusion, displacement, and movement between environmental affordances | Camera style does not make the encounter a head-to-head fighter. Sifu supplies a creator-described crowd-combat boundary case, not an uncontested taxonomy ruling. |
| Progression-heavy or equipment hybrid | Carry-over power, build decisions, supplies, and encounter scaling | Equipment progression can change which crowd decisions remain relevant; do not assume level count or loot confers strategic variety. |
| Cooperative party context | Shared space, ownership of opportunities, rescues, voluntary carrying, and mixed skill/access configurations | More active players need not mean the same solo encounter with larger enemy health. Equal damage is not synonymous with valued participation. |
| Repeated survival waves | Composition changes, environmental shifts, optional rewards, and persistence between attempts | Repetition can support practice; an authored variation count does not demonstrate new decisions or reduced boredom. |

The Nintendo mode example and the creator-described SoR4, TMNT, and Sifu structures support examining modes and mechanisms rather than treating labels as mutually exclusive. They do not establish the representative prevalence of these structures across the genre. [C-01–C-04]

## Custom research instructions

Begin with an **encounter-and-route contract**: player count and viewpoints; movement plane; targeting and facing; enemy roles and simultaneous opportunities; resource ownership; incapacitation/re-entry; and what ends or advances the encounter. Record the build or explicitly state that only an unversioned document or pre-release account is available. [C-01, C-05, C-10]

Research should challenge five propositions rather than assume them. [C-05–C-09, C-15]

1. **“More enemies create better crowd play.”** Ask whether a composition changes prioritization, movement, risk, and role interaction, or only time spent attacking. Inspect off-screen pressure, simultaneous attacks, escape opportunities, and the legitimacy of deliberate overwhelm.
2. **“Crowd control improves cooperation.”** Ask whose participation it enables and whose it removes. A long juggle, displacement, or immobilization can rescue a partner, monopolize targets, invalidate another player's action, or be an intentionally chosen support contribution. Do not preselect equal damage as the answer.
3. **“Shared camera creates shared experience.”** Test who determines progression, whether a lagging or downed player remains actionable, what information is outside the view, and how presentation changes with party composition. Distinguish a production account from an evaluated readability result.
4. **“Recovery makes the game forgiving.”** Separate spend-and-regain, injury recovery, checkpoints, lives, revives, continues, and team rescue. Examine how each affects resource opportunities, route pacing, social pressure, and interruption. Some recovery contracts can increase the burden on a struggling player.
5. **“Variety prevents repetition.”** Identify whether a changed encounter demands a different decision or only a different animation. Investigate repeated exposure and player goals, including the value of rehearsing a stable route. Seek cases where random or frequent change obstructs learning.

Use original rules/build documents for state transitions, firsthand creators for production reasoning, original evaluations for effects, and reproducible gameplay records for actual behavior. Require separate evidence for revival rules and friendly-fire conditions rather than filling them from memory. Seek single-player and cooperative cases, fixed and branching routes, planar and three-dimensional spaces, and different intended power fantasies. Broad genre audience expectations remain hypotheses until investigated. [C-15]

## Player promises and varied audiences

Candidate promises include controlling a threatening space, turning a group into manageable opportunities, expressing a physical character style, sharing a chaotic scene with friends, improving a practiced route, or sustaining a power fantasy. The intended experience may be carefully contested, permissively spectacular, or deliberately uneven across participants. These are possible design briefs, not universal player requirements. [C-05, C-06, C-15]

A partner who prefers assisting, observing briefly, or being carried is not automatically excluded from meaningful play. A partner who wants to act but cannot reach a target, see a rescue prompt, or retain input control may be experiencing a different problem. Ask about preferred contribution and observed opportunity separately. Participation design should not become compulsory productivity or a score-based judgment of a person's worth. [C-06, C-13]

## Characteristic loops and structures

### Crowd management and positional control

For analysis, trace **orient → group or separate threats → choose a target/action → commit → inspect remaining threats and space → reposition or recover**. This is a proposed model, not a universal animation sequence. The state to inspect includes enemy roles, approach directions, occupied and usable space, incapacitated actors, resource opportunities, and the route's next commitment. A single “enemy count” hides those differences. [C-05]

The SoR4 interview reports role combinations and co-op-specific adjustments. Sloclap's Sifu account describes displacement, targeting/camera, environmental use, and management of group aggression. These are practitioner accounts of production choices, not measured proof of fair or enjoyable crowd behavior. [C-03, C-04; GDR-SRC-20261010-G05-010; GDR-SRC-20261010-G05-013]

**Evidence-to-application bridge:** when multiple roles can interfere with one another or with player responses, composition is a plausible cause of encounter difficulty and choice. Therefore, compare candidate compositions at the level of *available responses*, not just health or count. Keep a stable baseline long enough to identify what a change causes. An alternative is a deliberately simple group that supports rhythmic mastery or spectacle; added role complexity has no automatic entitlement to production time. The proposed benefit remains untested here. [C-05]

For a belt-scrolling scene, inspect whether the player can infer depth alignment and facing before committing. For a three-dimensional arena, inspect orientation changes, occlusion, camera/targeting interactions, and environmental approach routes. For a hybrid with vertical traversal, test whether a platforming mistake consumes the same resources needed for subsequent crowd decisions. These are distinct applications of spatial information and commitment, not recommendations for a particular lane width, camera angle, or attack quota. [C-05, C-12]

### Crowd control and participation

Treat a crowd-control action as a change to **who can act, where, against what, and for how long**. Its proposed cooperative value could come from opening a rescue route or separating incompatible enemy pressures. Its cost could be denying a partner reachable targets, carrying an enemy beyond the shared frame, or repeatedly cancelling a partner's chosen action. A larger affected area is not necessarily a better team ability. [C-06]

Before tuning duration or damage, define the intended contribution: protection, setup, displacement, interruption, direct damage, or spectacle. Identify who can capitalize on it and whether that opportunity is intelligible. Compare with a simpler action that does less control but retains joint agency. Shared action ownership, release conditions, target reacquisition, and predictable end states are variables to investigate, not a required cooperative formula. [C-06]

**Counterexample:** if a group deliberately chooses one expert to perform an expressive sequence while others recover or watch, forced equal participation can spoil that goal. Conversely, calling unintended inactivity “support” does not resolve a participant's complaint. The discriminating evidence is the combination of preferred contribution, reachable opportunities, and observed interference—not damage equality alone. [C-06, C-13]

### Resources, incapacitation, and revival

SoR4's creators describe a special-action health cost that can be regained by attacking, with remaining recoverability lost when hit. This is the direct mechanism source for [Recoverable expenditure window](../patterns/recoverable-expenditure-window.md); it does not establish that the rule helps novices, cooperation, or every combat design. [C-03; GDR-SRC-20261010-G05-010]

Compare recovery contracts by writing the exact transition and opportunity: **what created the loss, what can restore it, who controls that opportunity, when it ends, and what persists afterward**. If restoration requires hitting a target, crowd composition and a partner's actions can affect restoration access. This dependency is a design inference; no G05 observation establishes how often that problem occurs in a shipped game. [C-07]

For revival, consider an automatic return, a self-spent resource, a partner rescue, a checkpoint restart, or no in-encounter revival. Each changes a different commitment. A partner rescue can create a temporary spatial objective but also put social pressure on the survivor. Requiring a sustained input can add a motor burden unrelated to rescue positioning. A resource-based return can preserve momentum while erasing a consequence the mode intends to retain. These are **illustrative alternatives**: the accessed sources did not verify a named game's exact revival, hold duration, or friendly-fire contract. [C-08]

Do not label every failed rescue as a coordination problem before checking camera reach, target selection, input usability, and the availability of a safe attempt. Do not assume that more lives fix unreadable attacks. At the route level, identify whether recovery resources are replenished, carried forward, consumed irreversibly, or unavailable after the last enemy. The cheapest defensible remedy may be a simpler restart contract rather than a more elaborate recovery economy. [C-07, C-08]

### Cooperative readability and shared-camera pressure

Tribute Games' TMNT co-op announcement reports planning player capacity alongside aspect ratio, character scale, and level scale. That supports treating presentation and composition as coupled production decisions; it does not prove a given number of players is readable. [C-04; GDR-SRC-20261010-G05-011]

Map which participant can see and act on each critical event. A shared camera may make distant actors smaller, constrain movement, or let a leading player determine when others lose access to a space. These are possible failure mechanisms to test, not observed results from the announcement. Distinguish enemies deliberately threatening from outside the frame from cues that simply omit essential direction. No blanket “never attack off-screen” rule is inferred. [C-06, C-12]

An alternative camera or separate views may reduce one kind of interference while creating a new communication or production problem. A stage-progression lock can protect a lagging player yet frustrate deliberate speed differences. Voluntary regrouping, explicit progression consent, bounded catch-up, or a different route structure are candidate alternatives; none is automatically optimal. Test interruption and re-entry as well as ideal coordinated play. [C-12, C-13]

### Repetition, stage progression, and route variety

The SoR4 interview describes Story-stage restart/assist distinctions and iterative encounter work. The TMNT DLC announcement describes a survival structure with changing waves/environments and reward-portal choices. These are documented structural alternatives, not evidence that one avoids boredom or that more authored waves produce more learning. [C-03, C-04; GDR-SRC-20261010-G05-010; GDR-SRC-20261010-G05-012]

For analysis, separate **perceptual variety** from **decision variety** and **practice stability**. A different enemy appearance can leave the same response dominant; changing geometry can alter what is safe even with familiar enemy roles. Conversely, replacing rules too often can make a learner unable to attribute improvement to a usable model. Stable repetition may be exactly the desired route-mastery experience. [C-09]

Use a repetition audit that asks what the player must notice, choose, coordinate, or retain differently. Compare a repeated composition with one changed role relation or spatial condition, not an uncontrolled bundle of new art, enemy health, and reward power. Where a progression system changes capabilities, re-evaluate whether old encounter roles still create distinct decisions. A fixed authored route can be preferable when reliability, accessibility review, or readable escalation matters more than combinatorial variety. [C-09, C-10]

## Pattern selection and adaptations

Use [Recoverable expenditure window](../patterns/recoverable-expenditure-window.md), `GDR-CAND-20261010-G05-PAT-02`, only when a deliberate expenditure creates the resource at risk. Check whether an ally can unintentionally exhaust the recovery opportunity, whether the last enemy closes it, and whether state presentation separates available from conditionally recoverable resources. Do not generalize the recipe into a revival system. Its claimed player benefit remains a low-confidence hypothesis. [C-07]

[Replay-to-variant practice](../patterns/replay-to-variant-practice.md), `GDR-CAND-20261010-G05-PAT-01`, can support a bounded crowd-decision explanation only when relevant actor states and continuations are represented. A two-actor replay can misteach a multi-actor encounter by omitting the next threat or a partner's choice. A simpler authored scenario using the existing F01 pattern may be less costly and more faithful. No inspected beat-em-up source establishes a shipped replay-takeover feature. [C-11]

The reviewed [P02 remapping](../reviews/full-run-20261010-g05-review-packet.md#dep-p02-remapping) and [cue alternatives](../reviews/full-run-20261010-g05-review-packet.md#dep-p02-cues) patterns apply to shared-screen identification and usable recovery controls, but neither promises complete cooperative accessibility. The [F07 balance-diagnosis pattern](../reviews/full-run-20261010-g05-review-packet.md#dep-f07-diagnosis) helps distinguish weak actions from unavailable or misunderstood ones. [C-10, C-12, C-14]

## Representative cases, worked example, and counterexamples

SoR4, TMNT, and Sifu are selected for **contrasting documented mechanisms and production contexts**, not market standing. TMNT's two articles share a development origin; its publisher overlaps with SoR4. The three creator contexts are not three independent demonstrations of efficacy. Nintendo's wave-mode document is a useful boundary counterexample, not evidence that all Smash modes belong in this entry. [C-02–C-04]

**Illustrative encounter — a rescue route or a monopolized target.** In a fictional corridor, one player can displace a group, another prefers close-range sequences, and a third is returning from incapacitation. An approaching threat makes displacement potentially useful for opening space. The same action may also carry the remaining targets beyond the second player's reach and move the action away from the returning player.

The proposed application is to record the intended cooperative function, identify the rescue route, and compare the current displacement with a smaller relocation or an earlier release. Hold enemy roles and resources stable for the first diagnostic contrast. Ask each person what contribution they intended, then inspect reachable actions and actual interference. A result where the group voluntarily prefers the long displacement is not a failure merely because contribution is unequal. A result where willing participants repeatedly have no actionable target challenges the participation claim. All events and outcomes in this example are hypothetical. [C-06, C-08, C-12, C-15]

**Hybrid conflict — gear growth versus readable roles.** A progression-heavy mode might make old enemies quickly disposable, fulfilling a power-growth promise but eliminating the group-management decisions used to teach an earlier stage. A candidate design could preserve selected role relations, introduce new ones, or deliberately accept the simpler encounter as a respite. The library cannot select the promise on behalf of a game. [C-09, C-10]

**Counterexample — deliberate spectacle.** Constant multi-role counterplay can be inappropriate in a mode intended for permissive, expressive crowd clearing. Conversely, a quiet route-mastery mode can benefit from repeated compositions. Neither is evidence that crowd design or variation is unnecessary everywhere; they show why a declared objective must precede the recommendation. [C-05, C-09]

## Accessibility, ethics, localization, and production implications

Distinguish characters and critical objects through actionable information rather than color alone. Test depth/facing comprehension without assuming that an outline solves occlusion or that a sound identifies who must respond. Cue alternatives should preserve the relevant actor, direction, eligibility, and uncertainty without exposing information that the source channel legitimately concealed. Richer output can increase attentional competition. These are conditional adaptations of corrected P01/P02 guidance, not a certification or universal cue specification. [C-12, C-14]

Audit simultaneous, repeated, and sustained activation separately from button assignment. Pay particular attention to rescue interactions, pickup/action conflicts, join/rejoin controls, and settings that affect more than one local player. Make restoration reachable through a usable configuration. Retain an accommodation during a learning probe unless changing it is the actual authorized research question. [C-11, C-14]

Localize relative directions, character identifiers, resource-state labels, revival prompts, and party-setting explanations. A world-relative “left,” a screen-relative arrow, and an actor-relative direction can imply different actions; establish the reference frame in the design. Do not infer cultural reception or representation approval from familiarity with a martial-arts theme. These are proposed checks; no localization or cultural-participant review was conducted. [C-13, C-14]

Production planning should account for actor combinations, camera states, resource edge cases, target ownership, revive/rejoin cases, accessibility configurations, and content updates. That is a potential testing burden, not a measured cost estimate. A fixed route or a smaller set of deliberate role combinations may be preferable to poorly explained variety. Do not create endless encounter or input-mode variants merely to satisfy a coverage list. [C-09, C-10, C-14]

For cooperative sessions, distinguish an honest commitment from an obligation enforced by unrelated punishment. Consider disclosed drop-out effects, supported handoff or re-entry, and the needs of remaining players. Do not infer that every mode must tolerate costless abandonment, or that a partner must accept unlimited rescue duty. These are ethical design questions, not legal advice or approved service policies. [C-13]

## Research questions and proposed validation

**Not performed.** Recruitment, game implementation, prototype execution, recordings, telemetry collection, and gameplay efficacy testing remain unauthorized in this research-only batch. Predefine the relevant population, configuration, outcomes, confounds, smallest worthwhile change, and stopping/rejection policy before any later study. Do not invent a fixed sample or acceptable inactivity threshold. [C-15]

| Question | Proposed comparison and procedure | Evidence that would narrow or reject the recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Does a role combination create a different useful decision? | Hold count, resource state, and geometry stable where meaningful; change a specified role relation. Compare predictions and chosen responses over repeat exposures. | If behavior changes only because damage or visibility changed, the role-synergy claim is not isolated. If the same dominant response remains and the desired objective is unchanged, added complexity may be unjustified. |
| Does crowd control support intended participation? | Compare a declared control action with a less restrictive alternative; include voluntary mixed-skill groups and document preferred roles before play. | Record reachable opportunities, forced inactivity, interference, rescues, and participant explanations—not just damage. Reject a participation claim when willing actors lose meaningful opportunities, while preserving legitimate preference for carrying or spectacle. |
| Does the resource recovery contract inform choice? | Compare a transparent recoverable expenditure with a fixed cost under an otherwise specified encounter. Include last-target, partner-interference, and stage-exit cases. | Reject the comprehension mechanism if players confuse recoverable with guaranteed resources. Reject deployment where recovery access depends on coercing partners or repeatedly creates unavoidable forfeiture contrary to the intended promise. |
| Is revival an actionable cooperative objective? | Contrast a specified partner rescue with a self-return or checkpoint alternative; test shared-camera and input configurations separately. | Distinguish refusal, inability, misunderstanding, and deliberate tactical delay. Reject a rescue design that is nominally available but unusable in decision-critical configurations. |
| Does variation help rather than obstruct learning? | Compare a stable repeated scene with a controlled role/spatial variant; observe first exposure and return after practice. | Track explanation, adaptation, preference, and recurrence of the same mistaken rule. Reject “more variety improves learning” when changes obscure causality or undermine the target route-mastery promise. |
| Does the camera preserve actionable cooperation? | Examine progression, separation, incapacitation, and rejoin states across intended party/view arrangements. | A larger frame is not sufficient if important actors or targets become unusable. Reject the framing claim where repeated loss of actor identification or rescue access occurs; investigate alternative views or progression contracts. |

## Coverage, review, and history

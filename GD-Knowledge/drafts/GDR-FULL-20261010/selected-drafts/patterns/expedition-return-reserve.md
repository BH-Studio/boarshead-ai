# Expedition return reserve

**Draft hypothesis-led recipe, not an established survival rule.** The proposed shared owner is Foundations and Systems; World and spatial composition, Presentation and Interaction, and applicable genre owners must review their boundaries. No ownership transfer, balancing target or game adoption has been approved. This is G14’s second and last new pattern in this packet. [C-01, C-08]

## Purpose

Make a resource commitment legible when an outward activity competes with reaching a **viable destination afterward**. That destination can be the starting shelter, another refuge or a recovery point; “return” must not force a circular journey that the game does not intend. The intended benefit is an informed choice, not maximally conservative play, a guarantee of survival, or a compulsory warning system. [C-01]

[C-02, C-08]

## Context and prerequisites

Apply when the player has a reason to continue beyond immediate safety, resources constrain the outward and onward journey, and there are meaningful alternatives such as shortening the trip, changing a destination, using a substitute or accepting a disclosed risk. Identify which capacities actually matter: resource quantity alone may be insufficient without a working route, permission, tool or shelter. [C-02]

The team must distinguish known cost, estimated range and unknown hazard; it must be able to explain which assumptions a route estimate uses. The player must have a usable way to obtain the relevant known information. This recipe does not require numerical displays if meaningful qualitative or accessible planning information serves the task. It does not prescribe a universal reserve percentage. [C-02, C-03]

**Do not start** when reaching a destination is not part of the objective, when resources never constrain continuation, or when the world is too indeterminate to support an honest bound. In the latter case, communicate uncertainty and alternatives rather than inventing a precise forecast. [C-03, C-07]

## Recipe

### 1. State the destination and continued activity

Specify what “viable afterward” means for the chosen objective: arrival, retaining observations, keeping a group operational, reaching a working recharge point, or accepting a defined recovery cost. Separate an optional safe-return preference from a mandatory mission requirement. The player may value a different outcome; do not diagnose that preference as a calculation error. [C-02, C-05]

**Exit:** if no intended continuation is defined, frame the activity first. A reserve with no purpose is just additional bookkeeping. [C-07]

### 2. Trace the resource and dependency path

Identify current usable stock, already committed work, routes, replenishment, storage access and dependencies. For each relevant resource, consider the chosen task and onward destination. Preserve separate constraints where capacities cannot substitute for one another; adding food, battery charge and carrying slots into one unexplained score can conceal an impossible route. [C-02]

A shelter can be safe from an enemy but unable to restore a resource. A cache can exist but be inaccessible. Another player can own supplies shown in a shared total. Mark these conditions explicitly in the model. They are illustrative risks to inspect, not claims about a particular game’s implementation. [C-02, C-06]

### 3. Define the reservation policy, not a universal margin

A possible illustrative policy is:

> Under the currently declared route and replenishment assumptions, discretionary capacity equals usable stock minus committed task cost, onward-travel provision and the chosen contingency.

This is an accounting identity **within that defined model**, not a survival guarantee. With interval estimates, show the range or the relevant conservative bound and label its assumptions. With unknown hazards, show that the model is incomplete. Do not substitute an invented probability or safety score. [C-02, C-03]

The player’s reserve may be a preference that can be knowingly overridden; a separate mission rule may forbid spending it. Do not silently convert one into the other. Where the intended activity is free exploration, the appropriate policy may be no reservation at all. [C-05, C-07]

### 4. Make the decision information usable

Show or otherwise convey the task’s additional commitment, the currently plausible destination and what an optional extension changes. A warning should explain the violated assumption or preference, not merely signal danger. Avoid answer-leading presentation during comprehension tests. In ordinary play, configurable planning aids can be appropriate; preserving access is more important than making every player do mental arithmetic. [C-03, C-05]

Reuse the reviewed accessibility and cue guidance rather than inventing new font, timing, haptic or audio prescriptions. If an estimate becomes obsolete, allow the player to recognize that fact before treating an old “safe” indication as current. [C-03]

### 5. Preserve alternatives and clarify loss

Candidate alternatives include turning back, reaching another viable point, changing the objective, discarding a burden, substituting a tool, resting, requesting assistance or accepting a disclosed loss. Their availability depends on the actual game; the recipe does not require every option. If depletion makes replenishment impossible, either disclose the terminal state or provide the promised recovery path. A nominal respawn without usable actions is not necessarily recovery. [C-04, C-06]

**Exit:** if only one compulsory action remains indefinitely and upkeep is not the desired activity, revise the pressure or remove the reserve mechanic. More warnings do not create meaningful alternatives. [C-07]

### 6. Update and test changed conditions

After a route, shelter, consumption rule or shared-store commitment changes, test whether the planning information follows. Include a change that alters the correct feasibility answer and a cosmetic change that does not. Ask for predictions before feedback; ask preference afterward. Preserve assistance when assessing transfer. [C-05]

**Adjustable variables:** destination, usable-resource scope, task commitment, replenishment conditions, estimate uncertainty, contingency policy, warning detail, override permission, time advancement, shared ownership and recovery consequences. Tune locally; no fixed reserve ratio, depletion rate, session duration or death frequency is supported here. [C-02–C-06]

## Worked examples

### Illustrative: an optional survey detour

This fictional example uses charge units, not real protective-equipment guidance. The explorer has 12 usable units. A required observation costs 2, onward travel to the declared shelter costs 6, and the player elects to protect 2 as contingency. The uncommitted amount is therefore 2. A detour costing 4 exceeds that policy by 2 units. [C-02]

If a verified alternative destination reduces onward cost to 2 while other values stay the same, 6 units are uncommitted before the detour. The same detour now fits, with 2 units beyond the protected contingency. If that destination’s recharge condition becomes unknown, the numerical fit no longer proves viability. All figures are invented to illustrate a changed answer, not game-balance recommendations. [C-02, C-03, C-05]

The player can decline either detour, spend a preferred reserve knowingly, or choose a different goal where permitted. An assessment asking “Can this preserve the stated reserve?” is not the same as “Do you want to proceed?” An assistance route might represent the same relationship using an inspectable allocation rather than mental subtraction. [C-05]

### Illustrative adaptation: a moving refuge

In a game about travel with a mobile shelter, the viable destination can move while the player explores. The model must account for the stated rendezvous policy; an obsolete route back to the departure point is misleading. A simpler design can hold the refuge stationary during excursions. Compare those alternatives before adopting the extra scheduling complexity. [C-02, C-06]

## Tradeoffs and alternatives

A conditional reserve can make resource commitments easier to inspect, but it may encourage unnecessary caution, create notification fatigue or turn discovery into compliance with a display. Detailed estimates also require reliable assumptions about routes, replenishment and time. These are hypotheses and model-based risks, not observed outcomes in the named games. [C-01, C-03, C-08]

Alternatives include generous resources with logistical decisions, automatic replenishment at declared intervals, short bounded excursions, a single resource tied clearly to a route, checkpoint recovery, or exploration without resource pressure. Use an existing configurable-consequences pattern when the actual problem is punitive loss rather than budget comprehension. Do less when it preserves the desired activity more clearly. [C-04, C-07]

## Failure cases

| Failure to test | Evidence to seek | Candidate correction |
|---|---|---|
| False guarantee | Participants infer “cannot fail” from an estimate that omits relevant hazards. | Change wording, uncertainty representation or scope; remove false precision. |
| Invisible committed stock | Shared resources appear available although reserved or inaccessible. | Separate ownership, access and commitments. |
| Circular recovery | Restoring the critical resource requires a depleted tool/resource. | Revise the dependency or clearly disclose the intended terminal outcome. |
| Compulsory caution | Participants understand the model but feel the system forbids chosen risk. | Distinguish preference from rule; permit an explicit override where appropriate. |
| Stale destination | Shelter or route changes invalidate the displayed reserve. | Update the model, flag obsolete information, or simplify moving dependencies. |
| Upkeep dominates the goal | The activity becomes repeated refilling with no valued choice. | Reduce upkeep or make calm maintenance an explicit supported activity rather than a presumed universal defect. |

The warning signs are proposed observation targets, not reported playtest findings. [C-03, C-05–C-08]

## Counterexamples and do not use conditions

A one-way journey need not reserve resources for the starting point. A game about intentionally exhausting a temporary body may make depletion a legitimate conclusion. Creative exploration may remove the budget entirely. A high-uncertainty narrative excursion may benefit from qualitative preparation rather than a numerical route forecast. These are illustrative counterexamples that can disconfirm the recipe’s applicability. [C-07]

The strongest opposing case is that exposing a reserve can undermine the very discovery the game values. Better understanding is not automatically better experience, and some players may prefer a less explicit policy while still needing usable access to the information the game provides. Test this conflict rather than presenting the reserve as a universal usability improvement. [C-08]

## Playtest

**Proposed, not performed.** Compare current stock-only information with stock plus a conditional onward-capacity representation. Keep actual world costs and alternatives matched; separately compare a reduced-upkeep design when the problem may be repetitive maintenance. Recruit by relevant familiarity and supported sensory, numerical, memory, motor and language needs. Do not impose a reserve preference as the “correct” player goal. [C-05, C-08]

Include adequate/inadequate variants with the same starting stock, a destination that changes cost, and an explicitly altered replenishment condition. Ask feasibility and remaining-uncertainty questions before feedback, then obtain the preferred action. Measure mistaken guarantees, policy comprehension, opportunity to recover, time spent on bookkeeping and the intended exploration experience. Counterbalance order and track route familiarity. No proposed participant count is presented as sufficient for statistical power. [C-05]

**Decision rule for local preregistration:** reject or adapt the technique if it increases false confidence, obscures the chosen objective, or constrains desired exploration without a practically worthwhile comprehension benefit. A simpler low-upkeep alternative may be preferable. Define meaningful differences, acceptable burden and recruitment before testing; report uncertainty and do not infer equivalence from a null result. [C-08]

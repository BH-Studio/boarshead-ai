# State-qualified recovery window

**Draft research candidate; not released or approved guidance.** All examples marked illustrative are invented, not studio canon. No game, engine, prototype, participant study or player test was run. Recommendations are conditional inferences or hypotheses, not formal standards.

## Purpose

Provide an opportunity to regain a **specified capacity** after an encounter, without mistaking a pause in newly introduced pressure for actual recovery. The intended capacity might be route orientation, review of a commitment, regrouping or another locally defined activity. The pattern does not promise relaxation, replenishment, safety, higher enjoyment or lower difficulty for everyone. Its assembled benefit chain is a hypothesis. [C-01]

This pattern’s distinctive decision is **when a proposed recovery opportunity is actually usable given outstanding demands**. It is not a general difficulty-adaptation system, a whole-session stopping policy or a promise that all choices preserve every resource. The simpler alternative is a fixed transition, continuous lower pressure, an explicit pause or no recovery stage at all. [C-02]

## Context and prerequisites

Apply only when the intended experience includes a recovery activity and the team can describe both that activity and the conditions that obstruct it. A fictional quiet room is insufficient evidence on its own. The scenario must support a relevant next state, and designers must be able to distinguish outstanding consequences from outstanding tasks that would consume the interval. [C-02, C-03]

| Prerequisite | What to establish | Exit when absent |
|---|---|---|
| A named recovery purpose | What the player should have an opportunity to do, and why now. | Omit the stage or describe it as another demanding phase rather than recovery. |
| Relevant outstanding-demand information | Which existing hazards, deadlines or commitments compete with the intended activity. | Use a more explicit bounded transition or narrow the promise; do not infer a person’s mental state from an unexplained score. |
| A supported way to reach the opportunity | How pressure can subside, be escaped or remain compatible with the activity. | Keep the scene unresolved or provide another supported route; do not create indefinite mandatory waiting. |
| Clear limits | What remains dangerous, costly or irreversible. | Remove misleading safety claims or change the interval so the claim becomes honest. |
| Feasible access and production scope | Relevant presentation, locale and continuity variants can be reviewed. | Reduce scope; do not silently leave some arrivals or access routes unsupported. |

These are proposed authoring prerequisites, not a formal platform standard or a validated diagnostic instrument. They require separate review before application. [C-02, C-08]

## Mechanism and evidence

| Proposed causal link | Current support | Limit and alternative explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Outstanding demands can consume a nominal lull. | Booth’s documented transition distinguishes existing combat from the relaxation phase. [C-03; GDR-SRC-20261010-W04-001] | One production account; not measured human recovery or a universal pacing schedule. |
| Identify the intended activity and its blockers → a more accurate recovery condition. | A dependency inference: an activity remains obstructed while its indispensable preconditions fail. [C-02, C-04] | The author may identify the wrong activity or blocker; latent anxiety and social load may remain. |
| Delay, relocate or qualify the opportunity → the activity becomes usable. | Proposed pattern step, not demonstrated evidence. [C-04] | Waiting may be boring; a fixed pause or simpler encounter can achieve the same thing more cheaply. |
| Communicate the interval’s limits → fewer mistaken expectations. | Proposed comprehension hypothesis. [C-05] | Extra explanation can overload the player or reveal an intended uncertainty. |
| Usable opportunity → recovered capacity or better subsequent play. | Unvalidated outcome hypothesis. [C-01, C-09] | The player may decline the activity, prefer continuous challenge or receive no benefit. Completion does not prove restoration. |
| Retain meaningful consequences → relief without nullifying the earlier decision. | Conditional design inference informed by inspected W01/W02/F07 candidate boundaries. [C-06] | Some designs intentionally rewind or replenish. Retention is not universally preferable. |

No inspected source compares this complete recipe with an appropriate control. No fixed duration, encounter count, recovery threshold or numerical “intensity” value is imported from the historical example. [C-03, C-09]

## Recipe

1. **Name the activity, not merely an emotion.** For example, “an opportunity to inspect the route and understand the remaining obligation.” Separately state the hoped-for experience; do not treat completion of the activity as proof that the person felt restored. [C-01]
2. **Trace relevant entry histories.** Include the arrivals the scenario actually supports: exhausted or available resources, remaining hazards, missing companions, skipped evidence, interruption and revisit. Do not expand this into an arbitrary exhaustive state model. [C-02]
3. **Identify residual competition.** Ask what would require attention or action during the opportunity. Distinguish a continuing consequence, such as an owed favor, from an urgent task, such as preventing immediate loss. Not every remembered consequence needs to vanish. [C-02, C-06]
4. **Choose how the opportunity becomes usable.** Options include waiting for a genuine lull, letting the player leave the active area, explicitly suspending a competing demand, reducing that demand or acknowledging that the scene remains pressured. Choose the smallest intervention that preserves the intended experience. [C-04]
5. **State what is and is not restored.** A route-review interval might preserve injury, debt or a changed relationship. A deliberate resource refill is a separate decision with its own consequences. Do not add one silently as though it were the same treatment. [C-06]
6. **Provide usable evidence of the limits.** Communicate remaining hazards or a deliberate protection boundary using an appropriate presentation plan. Preserve the same decision-relevant evidence across supported access routes; do not add certainty in one route that others intentionally lack. [C-05, C-08]
7. **Specify departure and re-entry.** Let the design explain what ends the interval, what happens if the player declines the activity, and how an interrupted or revisited scene differs. Avoid making completion of optional “rest” a hidden gate. [C-07]
8. **Compare, then retain or exit.** Evaluate the intended opportunity against a simpler alternative. If it adds unvalued waiting, false safety or maintenance cost without enough local benefit, revise or remove it. No result has yet been obtained. [C-09]

### Adjustable variables

Adjust the **recovering capacity**, included entry histories, which pressures remain, player control of departure, amount of contextual evidence, resource changes, consequence horizon and conditions for a new encounter. These are local design parameters. Changing several together creates a different treatment and weakens a claim that recovery timing alone caused an outcome. [C-04, C-09]

### Exit conditions

Stop applying the recipe when there is no valued recovery activity, when outstanding demands cannot be bounded honestly, when the stage requires indefinite waiting, or when it undermines an intentionally sustained challenge. Stop describing the interval as safe when exceptions remain. An explicit unresolved condition is better than a hidden false promise. Removing the pattern is an allowed outcome, not failed implementation of a universal law. [C-02, C-07]

## Worked examples

### Illustrative: after the storm-relay repair

The player has completed an invented relay repair. The local goal of the interval is to examine which route can deliver the report. A warning hazard remains active after the repair, and a helper may still need assistance. The intended next activity competes with those residual tasks. No prototype or playtest underlies this example. [C-10]

First, describe supported arrivals: the helper is present or absent; the report has or has not already been relayed through another station; the hazard is contained or still demands attention. The designer decides that a simple cue saying “take a moment” must not imply protection while the hazard remains active.

Second, compare possible responses. A fixed quiet interval begins immediately; a qualified interval begins only after the supported hazard resolution or escape; an explicit pause permits route review while stating what is suspended. These are three different candidate designs, not interchangeable implementations of one demonstrated best practice.

Third, retain the important consequences. A cell spent repairing the relay remains spent. An accepted favor remains owed. Those costs do not automatically obstruct reading the route, so the pattern does not erase them. If a fictional deadline continues, its effect remains apparent; suspending it would be a separate variant.

Finally, allow the player to depart without reading every optional entry. On return, the depot acknowledges whether the report was delivered instead of repeating an urgent request that no longer applies. The proposed evaluation distinguishes route understanding, mistaken safety assumptions, waiting, preference and later performance. A better route choice alone would not show that the player felt calmer.

### Illustrative: public hearing recess

After a demanding fictional hearing, a recess offers time to review what was actually agreed. If a reply deadline continues to expire during the review, the stage may still be pressured. The designer can disclose that continuing condition, suspend it or remove the recovery claim. A participant may prefer to continue immediately; mandatory recap could frustrate rather than restore them. This example transfers the same dependency question to a noncombat context, not a claim that all meetings require rests. [C-10]

## Tradeoffs and alternatives

A qualified transition may fit more histories but needs more review than a fixed scene. A fixed scene can be appropriate when the required prior state is guaranteed. Lowering continuous demands may help without introducing an explicit recovery stage. A player-controlled pause can address interruption needs but may have different consequences for a shared or time-dependent activity. An endurance challenge may intentionally preserve demand and should be judged against that declared purpose. [C-02, C-04, C-07]

The pattern does not inherit approval from the inspected F07 safe-exit draft. The person’s ability to stop, a character’s ability to retreat and a fictionally peaceful interval remain different design questions. Nor does it supersede W01 consequence-preserving reconvergence: that pattern owns the meaning of a retained difference across shared content; this one owns the usability of a specific interval. [C-06, C-07]

## Failure cases

**Nominal calm, continued work:** players spend the entire interval handling residual tasks. Correct by changing the claimed purpose, changing the tasks or choosing a different location/condition. **Infinite qualification:** the required state never arrives for a supported history. Provide another honest route or abandon the interval; do not hide a softlock behind an adaptive label. **False safety:** an existing exception interrupts a scene explicitly presented as protected. Revise the promise or the protection. [C-04, C-05, C-07]

**Recovery as compulsory administration:** the interval requires chores unrelated to the intended capacity. Observe what players actually do rather than infer rest from the scenery. **Resource erasure:** relief silently nullifies an important earlier cost. Review the persistence promise separately. **Access inequality:** one presentation route conveys the remaining deadline and another omits it. Review equivalent decision evidence before declaring the option usable. These are hypothesized failure modes, not measured incidence estimates. [C-06, C-08]

## Counterexamples and do not use conditions

Do not impose alternating relief on a scene whose agreed purpose is continuous pressure. Do not manufacture danger just to create contrast in an otherwise undisturbed experience. Do not force a long recovery interval on someone ready to continue merely because a model classified their behavior as stressed. This draft provides no evidence for such a mental-state classification. [C-02, C-07]

The strongest counterproposal is to make the encounter simpler. If a fixed transition or explicit pause accomplishes the same valued activity with fewer exceptions, the extra qualification may not be justified. The pattern should lose that comparison when its benefits do not exceed its costs under the local criteria. [C-04, C-09]

## Playtest

**Proposed, not performed.** In a later authorized prototype, compare a fixed transition, a state-qualified transition and a simpler pause/lower-pressure alternative where feasible. Keep route information, rewards, overall threat and traversal as comparable as possible; log unavoidable differences rather than call the comparison controlled. Use relevant participants across familiarity, language and access conditions without asserting a universal sample size. [C-09]

Ask participants to predict the interval’s remaining risks, then observe whether the intended activity is available and used. Record unwanted waiting, unresolved competing tasks, errors about consequences, preference and the relevant subsequent activity separately. Qualifying the interval is disconfirmed for the local use when it provides no practically meaningful improvement over the simpler option, or when waiting, misleading safety or exclusion is unacceptable. Define those local boundaries before the study; no numerical guarantee is supplied.

When checking comprehension of a prerequisite rule, include valid, invalid and boundary cases instead of rewarding a blanket refusal strategy. A transfer probe changes surface circumstances while retaining the underlying rule; a changed deadline or acceptance condition is an adaptation probe and must be reported separately. Counterbalance repeated exposure where possible and avoid equating absence of a significant difference with equivalence. These corrections were inspected in the selected F01/W02 bytes; no W04 behavioral test has run.

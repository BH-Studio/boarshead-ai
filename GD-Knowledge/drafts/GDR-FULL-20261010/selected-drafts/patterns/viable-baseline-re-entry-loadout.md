# Viable baseline re-entry loadout

**Draft 0.1.0 · initial-capture-1.** Proposed owner: BH-GDR-Foundations-and-Systems, subject to coordination with F03 economy/progression and relevant combat, traversal or task owners. This is a conditional recovery-equipment technique, not an approved economy design or a retention optimization rule.

## Purpose

Provide a **functional route into another voluntary attempt** when resource loss can otherwise remove the means to perform an intended role. Functional means the kit enables a consequential action in an identified baseline scenario. It does not mean equal gear, equal profit, a guaranteed victory or an obligation to play again. [C-03, C-04]

The recipe specializes the material entry floor and its risk/economy constraints. F07’s **Legible commitment and safe exit** owns informed participation and leaving; F02 owns challenge/recovery analysis. This pattern must not duplicate those broad principles or turn them into automatic replenishment for every game. [C-DEP]

## Context and prerequisites

Consider it when loadout loss or depletion can make subsequent participation impractical, and the intended experience is repeatable voluntary excursions rather than a finite resource campaign. Identify the task, necessary capabilities, resource exposure, ownership and what the player can retain. The baseline scenario must actually remain available to the depleted player. A free menu selection leading only to effectively impossible tasks does not establish viability. [C-03, C-04]

Prerequisites include a defined minimum role, a known route for obtaining baseline equipment without an unchosen purchase, understandable loss/return rules and an account of how equipment can enter or leave the persistent economy. “No purchase required for this proposed floor” is a condition of this recipe, not a claim that the source games satisfy every ethical or access requirement. [C-03–C-06]

**Entry stop condition:** do not add the pattern merely because a failure feels harsh. It may conflict with a deliberately finite expedition, meaningful communal reconstruction or a game in which everyone already resets to a viable state. Diagnose the actual missing capability first. [C-03, C-04]

## Mechanism and evidence

| Causal link | Support / classification | Limit and rival explanation |
|---|---|---|
| An entry option can supply equipment after depletion | Documented examples exist. [C-01] | The documents do not establish every eligibility rule or player outcome. |
| Supplied equipment enables a defined role | Conditional rule analysis; verify locally. [C-03] | A nominal kit can still be incapable, inaccessible or a burden shifted to teammates. |
| A functional role prevents material exclusion from that attempt | Inference once the capability and availability are demonstrated. [C-03, C-06] | It does not remove skill, knowledge, social or matchmaking barriers. |
| A baseline plus optional higher exposure preserves differentiated stakes | **Hypothesis**, motivated by differing documented conditions. [C-02, C-04] | The baseline may undermine intended risk or the restricted tier may become practically mandatory. |
| Players experience recovery without compelled return | **Hypothesis**, requiring direct evaluation. [C-04, C-05] | More attempts can reflect obligation or exploitation rather than benefit. |

No causal reduction in churn, compulsion, inequity or economic abuse has been demonstrated by G04. Two developers’ equipment descriptions are independent production contexts, not independent experiments validating this recipe. [C-01, C-04]

## Recipe

### 1. Diagnose the blocked participation state

Describe a concrete depleted state and the actions no longer feasible. Distinguish lack of a tool from lack of understanding, an inaccessible control, a locked mission or a social prerequisite. State whether the design intends recovery, temporary exclusion, reconstruction or a final ending. [C-03, C-06]

**Exit:** when depletion is not the cause, do not insert a free kit as a universal fix. When a finite ending is deliberate and understood, this pattern may be inappropriate. [C-03, C-04]

### 2. Define the minimum consequential role

Choose a baseline task and specify what counts as a real contribution: moving cargo, sensing a route, repairing an ally, securing an objective or interacting with a relevant threat. List required tools and access routes. Do not define viability solely as permission to enter or survival through teammates’ charity. [C-03]

**Decision point:** must every baseline player be self-sufficient, or can a voluntary team distribute capabilities? Record whose burden increases and whether that cooperation is part of the chosen promise. A support role is not inherently inferior, but it cannot be assumed acceptable without evaluating actual contribution and preference. [C-03, C-07]

### 3. Author the smallest usable baseline package

Supply only the capabilities needed for that role and disclose meaningful limitations. It could be a borrowed tool set, a standardized kit, a default vehicle or a recovery allocation; a gun is not required. Avoid cosmetic or inventory complexity that hides the relevant choice. Check that the player can select, understand, equip and use it through supported controls. [C-03, C-06]

**Exit:** if the package remains practically unusable in the baseline task, revise the package or the task. Do not label it viable based on cost alone. [C-03]

### 4. Separate optional risk tiers without hiding the floor

State which scenarios the baseline supports and which require an additional voluntary commitment. A high-stake route may demand capabilities or exposure absent from the floor, but it must not secretly become the only route to ordinary participation. Treat a restricted-tier source example as a question to evaluate, not proof that restricting access improves risk decisions. [C-02, C-04]

Adjustable variables include mission eligibility, carry capacity, role breadth, upgrade path, loss exceptions and whether baseline equipment is borrowed or retained. No mandatory cooldown, stash size, profit ratio or success percentage is proposed. [C-04, C-07]

### 5. Specify the material lifecycle and abuse boundaries

Record what can be retained, transferred, dismantled, sold, consumed, recovered or replaced, including interrupted attempts. Check whether repeating an entry action creates value disconnected from the intended task. Consider restrictions only against identified flows, then check who they would exclude. This is an economy threat-modeling question, not a recipe for exploiting a game. [C-05]

Avoid treating anti-duplication or compensation patch notes as evidence of a secure design. Actual transaction behavior, enforcement, false positives and support burden need separate implementation and review. No such tests were performed here. [C-05]

### 6. Make refusal and stopping genuinely separate choices

A player should be able to understand the accounting, stop, or choose a baseline attempt without a recovery-purchase prompt being framed as the necessary solution to loss. Do not define the pattern’s success as immediate requeue. Consider teammates’ already accepted commitments and state any bounds on leaving; a kit does not grant costless abandonment of an ongoing shared session. [C-05]

### 7. Evaluate, narrow or reject

Compare feasible contributions and burden under the actual baseline scenario, then separately assess comprehension, preference and voluntariness. Inspect the economy implications without recruiting players into harmful real-money loss. If the kit is nominally available but meaningfully excluded, or if it erases a deliberately chosen finite-risk premise, revise or reject the pattern. [C-03–C-07]

## Worked examples

### Illustrative example — salvage survey kit

A cooperative salvage mode lets a player lose a privately prepared equipment set. A baseline kit supplies a lamp, a basic carrier and a route-status reader. In an always-available introductory region, the player can retrieve a small but useful item or provide a relevant route report. The scenario and equipment are invented; no source game is being reconstructed. [C-07]

Define the role before tuning value: the reader must report a state someone can act on, the carrier must fit a task-relevant item and the route must be achievable with those tools. Make advanced regions optional rather than technically available but infeasible. State what returns to the lender and what the player retains. Audit whether transferred borrowed tools create unintended value, without assuming a specific restriction is automatically fair. After loss, stopping remains an acceptable outcome. [C-03–C-07]

### Illustrative adaptation — depleted maintenance drone

A noncombat repair game could provide a default drone capable of completing basic inspections after a specialized drone is lost. It need not perform every profitable task. A route requiring an advanced tool remains an explicit separate commitment, while the baseline inspection remains consequential. The principle is feasible participation, not equipment equality. [C-03, C-04, C-07]

### Documented limit, not a recommendation

Embark’s dated notice describes a temporary free-loadout restriction in selected high-loot conditions. It challenges a rule that a baseline must be valid in every tier. It does not establish that the restriction improved outcomes or that the same restriction should exist in a new design. [C-02]

## Tradeoffs and alternatives

A standardized reset can be simpler when persistent equipment is unnecessary. Training or a lower-exposure mode can address knowledge without changing the main economy. Partial retention, insurance or communal rebuilding can preserve different consequences, but each has different beneficiaries and incentives. A finite campaign ending may be preferable when voluntary acceptance of depletion is the intended premise. None is interchangeable with supplying a baseline kit. [C-03–C-05]

The kit costs authoring and balance work: tasks where it contributes, interfaces that disclose constraints, lifecycle accounting and review of interactions with upgraded gear. Too much capability can erase preparation tradeoffs; too little can create a nominal free path whose practical function is to advertise paid or high-grind recovery. These are risks to examine, not observed outcomes in the cited games. [C-04–C-07]

## Failure cases and do-not-use conditions

| Failure | Observable warning sign to investigate | Proposed response |
|---|---|---|
| Queue eligibility mistaken for viability | A depleted player can enter but lacks any tool that changes a relevant task. | Redefine the role, improve the package or narrow the scenario claim. [C-03] |
| Burden shifted to others | Teammates must subsidize every consequential action, contrary to the intended role. | Review voluntary interdependence and actual contribution, not just average survival. [C-03, C-07] |
| Baseline becomes an economic shortcut | Value can accumulate without the intended task contribution. | Review lifecycle flows and restrictions with the economy owner. Do not invent exploit-proof assurances. [C-05] |
| Restriction makes the floor illusory | The only practical progression or participation path requires an unavailable commitment. | Restore a consequential baseline path or disclose and revise the intended audience/promise. [C-04] |
| Recovery creates pressure | People believe they must immediately re-enter to repair their loss or avoid another penalty. | Review stopping, messaging and retention incentives separately. [C-05] |
| Accessibility missing | Selection or use fails in a claimed input/access configuration. | Fix the full task, not just equipment cost. [C-06] |

Do not use the pattern to prolong play for its own sake, to disguise a purchase requirement, to promise fair matchmaking, or to override a knowingly chosen finite-loss structure. No single recipe resolves all access, fairness and challenge tensions. [C-03–C-06]

## Proposed playtests — not performed

**P-B1: Functional floor.** Compare the defined depleted-state kit with the ordinary baseline task and a simpler recovery alternative. Use a common finite endowment, equivalent rules and known task requirements. Include different experience levels, supported inputs and relevant solo/team configurations. Measure achievable contribution, blocked actions, burden on others, understanding of retained/lost assets and preference. Inspect source/sink flows conceptually; no live exploit or participant economy test is authorized by this document. [C-03, C-05–C-07]

**P-B2: Meaningful risk without compelled return.** Compare a baseline-only presentation with a clearly separated optional risk tier. Ask prediction before preference, including scenarios where eligibility and sensible commitment differ; preserve access aids and changed-answer transfer cases. Record informed refusal and stopping as valid choices. Treat squads as interacting units, record wealth/skill/familiarity/time constraints, and obtain consent for fictional stakes. [C-04, C-05, C-07]

**Falsification and exit:** reject the floor claim when the kit cannot perform any intended consequential action, depends on an unchosen purchase, or shifts unacceptable mandatory burden to others. Reject the stronger welfare claim when participants misunderstand loss or report pressure despite higher participation. If the alternative recovery model better preserves the intended finite or collective challenge, choose it. Predefine context-specific criteria and justify sample size separately; no numerical threshold or causal success is claimed. [C-03–C-07]

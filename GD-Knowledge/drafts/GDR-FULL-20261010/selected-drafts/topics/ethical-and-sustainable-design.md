# Ethical and sustainable design

**Draft research candidate, not released guidance.** This entry supplies conditional design reasoning, not a legal opinion, clinical assessment, sustainability certification or approved studio policy. No players, purchases, prototypes, telemetry, energy measurements or workplace tests were used in this batch.

## Decision and scope

The decision is whether a proposed rule, incentive, payment structure, representation or production commitment offers a defensible experience **for the people affected, under the actual choices and constraints available to them**. The scope includes players, people represented, collaborators, support staff and production capacity. Environmental performance is included as a bounded additional dimension, not substituted for human sustainability. [C-01]

Working terms: **agency** means having understandable, usable choices within the offered experience; it does not require costless victory. **A coercive-risk mechanism** is a design arrangement that may pressure participation, payment or disclosure against a person's considered preference. This functional label avoids claiming a creator's intent where intent is unknown. **Wellbeing** refers to the particular constructs and instruments in an identified study, not a score inferred from engagement. **Sustainable production** means a proposed commitment can be maintained within explicitly accepted labor, maintenance and resource constraints; it is not a claim that a studio has achieved sustainability. [C-01, C-02]

No genre convention automatically defeats an ethical concern. Conversely, difficulty, uncertainty, horror, fictional deception or a willingly chosen competitive commitment is not automatically coercion. The relevant distinction is between the understood experience and concealed or practically unavoidable costs outside that understanding. Disagreement about that boundary must remain visible. [C-02, C-03]

## Core explanation and mechanisms

### Audit both what is offered and how refusal works

An incentive offers a reason to act. Risk increases when its real cost is concealed, the choice is time-pressured, declining adds punitive friction, prior investment is threatened, or another person's expectations make exit unusually costly. These are **proposed mechanism questions**, not a claim that each feature necessarily causes harm. Examine the sequence before commitment, during participation, at refusal and on return; a clear first screen does not settle the rest of the experience. [C-03]

Zagal, Björk and Lewis's dark-pattern concept distinguishes intentional exploitation from merely poor design and considers player knowledge and consent. This makes intent a limitation of that particular definition. In practice, a team can investigate a risky outcome without asserting intent or waiting for it to be proved. “Risk identified” and “developer deliberately deceived players” are different claims. [C-02, C-03]

Petrovskaya and Zendle analyzed 1,104 self-selected survey responses about perceived unfair, misleading or aggressive monetization. Those reports help generate questions about purchases and expected value; they do not verify hidden algorithms, represent all players, or establish causal harm. The recruitment and wording favored negative accounts, and demographics were not collected. [C-04]

The design implication is not “all monetization is unethical.” It is to test whether people can understand and decline an offer and whether the complete rule system is compatible with the offered value. Disclosure without a usable alternative can leave pressure intact; a purchasable solution can also be a legitimate, valued option. Both possibilities require examination. [C-03, C-05]

### Wellbeing evidence is not a retention optimization target

Vuorre and colleagues studied 38,935 active adults across seven games using publisher play records and three survey waves over six weeks. Their average modeled effect of an extra daily hour on affect was near zero, with a 95% credibility interval of −0.09 to 0.16 on their 13-point scale. Selection, attrition, the two-week lag and causal assumptions limit interpretation. This does not establish safety of every game, mechanic or intensity of use. [C-06]

Egami and colleagues studied pandemic-era Japanese console-purchase lotteries. Among 8,192 lottery participants within a larger survey, winning a Switch lottery was associated with a model-estimated distress reduction of 0.18 standard deviations (95% confidence interval 0.13–0.24) under their natural-experiment assumptions. This estimates a particular access opportunity, not the effect of a retention reward. Their identification and selection limitations remain relevant. [C-07]

These findings are not opposite answers to one identical question. Exposure, population, period, measurement and causal assumptions differ. Neither justifies maximizing session duration, prescribing a universal play-time threshold, diagnosing players from logs, or marketing a specific mechanic as therapeutic. Research on aggregate play does not validate a local incentive mechanism. [C-08]

The 2018 Zendle–Cairns survey links loot-box spending and problem-gambling questionnaire categories in a self-selected adult sample; causal direction was not determined. Its 2019 correction removes the original claim that a classification explained 37.7% of spending variance. The correction is retained with the source and that number is not used as a finding. This is a reason for scoped caution, not a clinical diagnosis or a legal classification of any product. [C-09, C-10]

### Representation is a relationship and a production decision, not just a count

GLAAD's Nielsen-supported survey included 1,452 active US PC/console gamers, with an LGBTQ sample boost. On the report's representation question, 72% of LGBTQ respondents said feeling well represented made them feel better about themselves. That is a self-report, not a randomized wellbeing effect, a global estimate or a sales forecast. Other identity and cultural questions remain incompletely covered by this evidence. [C-11]

A firsthand Never Alone account describes community collaboration and permission in adapting cultural material. Its value here is a concrete production route, not proof that any consultant or single permission-grantor represents an entire community. Representation review also concerns actions, rewards, available roles, who has agency, and whose objection can cause a revision—not only names, images or dialogue. The latter review questions are this entry's proposed transfer. [C-12, C-13]

### Sustaining a promise has human and environmental costs

The IGDA/Western University 2023 survey's final valid sample contained 777 responses; 28% of respondents to the relevant item reported crunch, with another 25% reporting extended hours not called crunch. The voluntary sample and varying denominators prevent treating those figures as universal industry rates. The survey does not demonstrate that a particular content cadence causes or cures overwork. [C-14]

The practical inference is to price a feature's continuing obligations before promising it: authoring, localization, access QA, compatibility, moderation, customer support, correction and eventual retirement. A smaller repeatable scope can be preferable to frequent novelty, but only if it still supplies the intended player value. Do not move costs from developers to unpaid moderators, contractors or players and call the burden eliminated. [C-15]

Microsoft's Xbox guidance recommends profiling distinct game states and explains its device-specific relative Power Load metric. It identifies rendering hidden behind full-screen menus as an opportunity to investigate. That is not a measured saving for this studio or a universal frame-rate prescription. A quality, accessibility or responsiveness regression can outweigh a poorly chosen optimization. [C-16, C-17]

## Design choices and alternatives

| Decision | Proposed lower-risk option and reason | Cost, opposing case and what still needs testing |
|---|---|---|
| Time-based rewards | Let rewards support chosen sessions; consider accumulation, a selectable window or a non-expiring progression route | Scarcity or a shared schedule may be part of the promise. Removing it can reduce coordination or meaningful commitment; inspect who benefits and who bears the burden |
| Paid offers and currencies | Make the actual offer, total transaction cost, contents, uncertainty and residual balance legible before confirmation; provide a usable refusal route | More explanation can overload. Real-money equivalents depend on the actual funding path; do not display a misleading universal conversion when bundles vary |
| Randomized paid rewards | Consider a known-content purchase or a non-paid route when randomness is not essential to the experience | Predictability changes collecting and revenue possibilities. A disclosed random offer is not thereby proven safe; a known-content offer can still exert pressure |
| Challenge and assistance | Preserve the intended decision while offering appropriate ways to learn, recover or adjust execution demands | Some communities value standardized competition. Compare like conditions or separate rule sets rather than falsely promising identical outcomes |
| Social commitment | Make commitment length and effects on others clear; provide planned stopping points, handoff or bounded reconnection where feasible | Unrestricted mid-match exit can harm others or become an exploit. Preserve proportionate, disclosed consequences and non-abusive recovery options |
| Representation | Pay relevant contributors for review, define revision influence, record disagreements and permission boundaries | Consultation takes time and does not create universal consent. “One consultant approved it” can hide substantial disagreement |
| Ongoing content/service | Choose a cadence with maintenance, access, localization and support capacity; consider fewer deeper updates or a finite complete experience | Reduced cadence can weaken a service promise. A plan must fund existing commitments and be honest about what will end |
| Energy use | Identify states doing unnecessary work; profile any change against experience and access constraints | Input inactivity may be active reading, assistive use or spectating. Do not darken, throttle or suspend solely from a naïve last-input assumption |

These are alternatives to investigate, not a checklist that certifies ethics. The causal proposals are grouped under C-03, C-05, C-13, C-15 and C-17. Their benefits, revenue effects and cultural reception have not been measured by this batch.

## Application process

1. **Map the promise and affected people.** Record the experience offered, purchase/time/data commitments, third-party obligations, represented communities and ongoing staffing needs. Note who may be absent from ordinary feedback channels. [C-01, C-13, C-15]
2. **Trace the whole commitment.** Walk through discovery, first offer, understanding, payment or enrollment, actual value, refusal, cancellation/interruption, recovery and re-entry. Identify the cost in each state and who controls it. [C-03, C-05]
3. **Write competing explanations.** For repeated participation, compare enjoyment, convenient habit, social coordination, lack of an exit, misunderstanding and fear of losing prior progress. For complaints, consider both a genuine mismatch and an understood but disliked rule. Counts alone cannot choose the explanation. [C-03, C-08]
4. **Establish non-negotiable constraints before optimization.** Propose clear consent, accessible explanations, privacy minimization, no deliberately deceptive payment interactions, and no experimental exposure to credible serious harm merely to estimate conversion. The receiving project must approve its actual constraints and assessment route. [C-18, C-19]
5. **Choose a minimally burdensome alternative.** Compare options from the table with the original player promise, not just revenue. Define a smaller acceptable scope when ongoing staffing or evidence cannot support the full proposal. [C-05, C-15]
6. **Plan falsification, not a favorable testimonial.** Ask whether people can predict cost, decline, stop and return; measure experience separately from usage. Record subgroup disagreement, support burden and longer-horizon uncertainty. Ethical constraints are not waived by statistical significance. [C-08, C-19]
7. **Review representation and production dependencies.** Bring narrative/character ownership to relevant communities' contributions and presentation ownership to comprehensibility/access. Give reviewers a concrete ability to request change. Do not claim these other Projects were consulted here. [C-13, C-15]
8. **Record a bounded decision with an exit.** Keep, modify, omit or defer the feature; identify remaining risks, decision owner and review trigger. Changes in pricing, audience, event cadence, platform or ongoing support reopen the relevant review. Research completion does not implement this process. [C-19]

## Examples and counterexamples

### Illustrative example: a reward calendar without a hidden obligation

A proposed system offers a reward after a sequence of optional tasks. Missing a day originally erases the sequence, and the task menu places a paid recovery next to the loss notice. No user results are assumed. The risk hypothesis is that people return primarily to avoid losing accumulated effort rather than because the next session is wanted.

An alternative separates earned progress from calendar attendance. The task description explains what carries forward, the reward preview specifies contents, and a player can stop without losing completed work. A genuinely shared event can retain its published end time while offering a plainly stated alternative for people who cannot attend. The design should not disguise an alternative as equivalent when its reward or required effort differs.

A proposed comparison asks participants to explain the consequences before acting, then choose to play, defer or decline in a no-money mock scenario. Later separately authorized observation would be needed to learn whether the alternative changes felt pressure in ordinary life. A lower return rate could mean less pressure, less appeal, or both. It is not automatically a failure or an ethical success. If the revision removes a voluntarily valued shared ritual, seek a selectable commitment rather than imposing the author's preference. [C-03, C-05, C-08, C-19]

### Illustrative example: a purchase whose apparently clear price is still confusing

An offer costs 300 tokens. A mock account has 200, and the smallest top-up supplies 250. A participant must understand both the offer's token price and the additional transaction required, including the remaining balance. These numbers are invented and not recommended prices. The test is not whether a token icon is recognized; it is whether the complete purchasing consequence can be explained before confirmation. An alternative known-price direct purchase may simplify the commitment, but compatibility with an existing economy and outstanding balances requires separate design review. No real purchase is authorized here. [C-05]

### Illustrative example: sustainable scope without outsourcing the burden

A team proposes a frequent rotating event with translated text, new rewards, moderation and special support cases. A capacity record counts all of those obligations, not just initial authoring. A smaller reusable event with reviewed variants could preserve the central decision while reducing production uncertainty. The opposing case is that repetition undermines the actual promise; then an honestly finite experience may be more defensible than an unsupported service cadence. The proposal has not been tested against work hours, retention or revenue. [C-15]

### Counterexample: “safe exit” cannot mean externalizing every cost

In a shared commitment, leaving can affect other people even when the interface is truthful. A rule that refunds all costs immediately can enable repeated avoidance of unfavorable situations. The lower-risk design question is whether consequences are known, proportionate, accessible and bounded, with recovery for disconnection or emergency where feasible—not whether leaving must be costless in every circumstance. Conversely, threats to erase unrelated earned progress cannot be justified merely by calling the activity competitive. [C-03, C-19]

## Failure modes and constraints

**Ethics by dashboard.** A low complaint count can reflect poor reporting access, a small vulnerable population or silence. An increased purchase rate may be caused by confusion. Treat outcome metrics as evidence with an interpretation problem, not as ethical permission. [C-03, C-08]

**Disclosure as absolution.** Dense terms, unstable prices, misleading comparisons, inaccessible text or refusal buried behind extra steps can defeat meaningful understanding. The recommendation is to test the actual choice, including the refusal path, not to count disclosed facts. This is a falsifiable usability and agency hypothesis; no local benefit has been measured. [C-05, C-19]

**Paternalism and overgeneralization.** Players can knowingly choose difficult, emotionally demanding or time-bound experiences. Do not treat dissatisfaction as a diagnosis, nor remove valued commitments solely because the author prefers frictionless play. Children, people under financial stress and people with specific access needs are not adequately represented by the inspected adult or general panel evidence; do not extrapolate safety to them. No child-facing research protocol is supplied. [C-02, C-06–C-09, C-19]

**Representation as a waiver.** A demographic count does not settle portrayal, authority or unintended rule incentives. One contributor cannot certify an entire group, and requiring affected people to educate a team without support can add burden. Broader evidence on race, disability, religion, language, regional identity and intersectional experiences remains a gap. [C-11–C-13]

**Environmental overclaim.** Reduced device power in one state is not a measured reduction in whole-service emissions. Specify hardware, duration, workload and measurement boundary. Energy depends on power over time; a carbon claim additionally needs a defensible accounting boundary and emissions factors. Lower momentary draw does not imply lower total energy if activity expands enough. These are dimensional and scope distinctions, not a lifecycle study. [C-16, C-17]

**Production wishful thinking.** Reuse, automation and fewer events may reduce one task while adding integration, correction, review or moderation elsewhere. No “crunch-free,” “carbon-neutral” or clinical benefit label is established by this packet. [C-14, C-15, C-17]

## Playtest and validation

**PROPOSED / NOT PERFORMED.** No recruitment, real spending, exposure to deceptive offers, diagnosis or workplace experiment is authorized. A future team must obtain approval, define safeguards, and match the method to the sensitivity of the question.

| Question and comparison | Measures and relevant participants | Confounds and falsifying result |
|---|---|---|
| Does a legible offer improve informed choice? Compare two non-transactional mock flows with the same stated value | Ask relevant language/access groups to explain total cost, uncertainty, decline route and residual balance before choosing; observe assistance and accidental commitment | Prior currency experience, reading load and moderator cues. Reject the proposed revision if it merely adds text while important misunderstandings or accidental actions persist |
| Does an interruption alternative preserve wanted participation with less pressure? Compare selectable timing/progress rules in a safe hypothetical task | Separately measure comprehension, preference, perceived obligation and intended stopping; later naturalistic experience needs separate approval | Hypothetical responses do not establish real behavior. Adapt the proposal if it removes a valued commitment or transfers burden to collaborators |
| Does representation review change problematic system implications? Review alternative roles, reward structures and descriptions with compensated relevant contributors | Record specific interpretations, contested readings, requested revisions and actual decision influence; avoid a single averaged approval score | Reviewer selection, tokenism and power imbalance. Reject the review process as sufficient if contributors identify a material issue but cannot influence the decision or if key perspectives remain missing |
| Can a content promise be maintained responsibly? Review a scoped production forecast rather than staging overwork | Record estimated work, uncertainty, maintenance ownership, access/localization/support capacity and explicit deferral choices | Estimates are not observed workload. Reduce scope or withhold the promise if it depends on unaccepted overtime or unowned continuing duties |
| Is an energy-saving candidate worthwhile? Future engineering comparison of matched device states with and without the change | Record appropriate power/energy measures plus responsiveness, readability, assistive behavior and return-from-idle performance | Device, thermal state, scene, duration and inactivity classification. Reject or revise if savings cannot be isolated or necessary experience/access degrades |

The Belmont Report informs the proposed consent and participant-burden safeguards, especially comprehension and voluntariness. It is an identified normative ethical report, not an assertion of product-law applicability. A refusal to run a harmful comparison is a defensible design boundary, not a failed experiment. [C-18, C-19]

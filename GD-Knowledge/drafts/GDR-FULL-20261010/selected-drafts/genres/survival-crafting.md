# Survival crafting

> **Bounded research candidate, not released knowledge.** This is the separate G15 genre entry, not a replacement for Survival or a genre-neutral crafting/economy topic. Its causal recommendations are hypotheses. No gameplay session, prototype, participant study or implementation test was performed. Public examples below are observations of the specified official documents, not firsthand gameplay observations.

## Decision and scope

Use this entry to decide how acquiring and transforming materials changes a player's capacity to continue, explore, construct and pursue chosen projects over a persistent world history. The working definition emphasizes **provisioning through making**: a resource or process becomes a tool, structure, consumable or capability, which changes later possibilities and obligations. It is a proposed analytical boundary, not an approved studio taxonomy or an assertion that every marketed survival-crafting game has the same loop. [C-01; GDR-SRC-20261010-G15-001; GDR-SRC-20261010-G15-002; GDR-SRC-20261010-G15-003]

The relevant decisions are acquisition versus transformation, discovery versus reacquisition, tool and station dependence, resource renewal, repair, world persistence, exploration gates, personal projects and shared progress. Combat statistics, server implementation, comprehensive economy design and the whole Survival genre remain with their respective owners. A hostile world, hunger meter, particular camera, procedural map, online server or competitive mode is not imposed as a membership requirement. [C-01]

## Definition and contested boundaries

**Survival versus survival crafting.** A survival design can make continuing under environmental or resource pressure its central problem without giving material transformation a large role. This entry adds the distinctive dependency between making capacity and future survival/exploration options. A game can cross that boundary at different stages: an opening focused on gathering may become an infrastructure game, while a late expedition may restore vulnerability. Do not replace the missing parent Survival entry with this conditional definition. [C-01]

**Sandbox and creative construction.** A construction activity can prioritize expression without provisioning pressure. Minecraft's official framing explicitly allows different goals and distinguishes Creative and Survival modes; it supplies a negative case against treating every activity in a craft-enabled game as a survival problem. The name of the game is therefore insufficient evidence for the current activity. [C-01, C-12; GDR-SRC-20261010-G15-002, §§goal/modes]

**Management, automation and role-playing.** Direct collection, delegated production and abstract purchase can all supply a project, but they allocate attention differently. A crafting profession in a role-playing game does not by itself establish this genre's persistent provisioning structure. Likewise, an automation-heavy hybrid may move the interesting choice from performing work to designing throughput. These are analytical distinctions to investigate, not claims that direct labor is more authentic or that automation necessarily improves play. [C-01, C-08]

**Tone and social arrangement.** Cozy presentation, harsh fiction, solo play, cooperation and PvP are facets. A friendly-looking resource chain can still create compulsory maintenance; a threatening setting can support forgiving loss rules. No label establishes actual access, ethical quality or preferred hardship. [C-01, C-11–C-13]

## Custom research instructions

Research a **specific material-to-capability chain**, not a shopping list of features. For each representative build, record the resource's acquisition conditions, what consumes it, required station/tool/knowledge, whether outputs persist, and how a player can restart after losing the relevant means of production. Separate authored recipe knowledge, world facts, personally learned knowledge and community-distributed knowledge. A recipe being visible does not mean its ingredients are reachable. [C-06, C-07]

Select contrasting cases: authored and generated geography; fixed recipes and more expressive construction; noncombat provisioning and combat-gated materials; finite and renewing supply; solo saves, transferable hosting and persistent servers; familiar and unfamiliar players; players returning after absence; users of alternative inputs and information routes. These are **sampling targets**, not populations already studied here. The available corpus disproportionately covers documented commercial PC/console examples. [C-12–C-14]

Challenge misleading comparisons. Holding nominal recipe cost constant does not hold effort constant when travel, carrying, danger or conversion changes. A shorter gathering session is not automatically more agency; a longer one is not automatically engagement. A successful game, official convenience feature or enthusiastic community cannot establish that repeating labor causes satisfaction. When an update changes a resource system, distinguish the old and new rules before interpreting player reports. [C-06, C-08, C-14]

Primary-source routes should include versioned recipe and save rules, creator explanations, patch histories, actual state observations with reproducible build/route information, and original studies with appropriate methods. The inaccessible *Crafting in Games* lead is recorded, not used to import an unread classification. Requests for universal material ratios, repair prices or time-to-upgrade targets should be rejected or reframed as proposed local parameters. [C-14]

## Player promises and varied audiences

Treat the following as candidate goals to elicit, not genre-wide expectations: “my preparation makes another place reachable,” “my home records what I made,” “I can discover how materials work,” “our different projects can coexist,” and “I can return without an unpaid maintenance backlog.” A person can value several, change priorities during a session, or prefer the repetitive work itself. Do not equate a preference for routine gathering with poor skill, nor a preference for building with dislike of survival. [C-12]

The decision is whether the particular chain serves the chosen goal. For expedition planning, carrying and durability can make loadout choices consequential. For architectural expression, those same costs may repeatedly interrupt the target activity. For group projects, a shared stockpile may enable contribution but obscure who has promised which materials. These possible effects require observation and discussion; current documentation demonstrates available designs, not how many players want them. [C-08, C-11, C-12]

## Characteristic loops and mechanisms

### Acquisition, transformation and capacity

An illustrative loop is **choose a project → locate or obtain materials → transform them under prerequisites → use the output → maintain or revise the world → choose again**. The important state change is not simply the inventory count: an acquired tool may open a new material source, and a station may convert previously useless stock into a viable option. Minecraft's introductory guide documents a simple material-to-workstation chain and recipe lookup, but does not validate this larger motivational interpretation. [C-02, C-06; GDR-SRC-20261010-G15-001, §§Starting to Craft/Crafting Recipes]

Before modifying yields, diagnose what stops progress. Is the player missing the recipe identity, the location, physical access, the quantity, a conversion prerequisite, storage access or permission to consume shared stock? Increasing abundance addresses only some of those conditions. It can leave a hidden station dependency intact while weakening a valued expedition decision. This is a proposed diagnostic method, not a measured optimization result. [C-06]

### Dependency chains, bootstrap and renewal

Represent essential production as conditional relationships, including alternatives and consumables. A station may need a tool made at another station; a fuel may require access opened by the product it powers. A valid opening sequence does not prove post-loss recoverability. Review the last-tool, destroyed-station, exhausted-local-node and absent-specialist cases separately. A logical continuation also does not demonstrate that the player can identify or execute it. [C-07]

Finite supply and renewable supply need not be permanent opposites. Grounded 0.14.0 notes describe finite upgrade finds followed by crafting unlocks, while retaining a then-current Rad Nugget exception. This is a concrete historical variation, not an argument that every chain should become renewable. A finite stock can support irreversible allocation; renewal can support experimentation or rebuilding. The design question is which commitments remain meaningful after the transition. [C-04, C-08; GDR-SRC-20261010-G15-005, §Armor and Weapon Upgrade Changes]

### Discovery, reacquisition and world persistence

First discovery and later replenishment can be different activities. Grounded 0.12.0 documents surveying analyzed materials within activated coverage; a later update moved the activation switch. That is evidence for an authored lookup mechanism with progression conditions, not proof that it preserves every player's discovery experience. Personal exploration and station coverage are not identical sets. [C-03; GDR-SRC-20261010-G15-004, §Resource Survey Stations; GDR-SRC-20261010-G15-005, §World/Resources]

Consider optional lookup after the relevant discovery only when repeat search is the diagnosed burden. An information aid does not create missing material, unlock a route, shorten a smelting queue or grant permission to use another player's supply. The new [Discovery-scoped resource survey](../patterns/discovery-scoped-resource-survey.md) therefore includes disclosure, freshness and exit policies, not just a map marker. [C-06, C-09]

### Session and social horizons

Persistent world state, host availability, offline simulation and group permission are separate questions. Iron Gate's guide describes dedicated-world hosting and independent pressure modifiers. Grounded's historical Shared Worlds notes describe transferable hosting with a single active host and explicitly warn of beta save risk. Neither description establishes that a group has agreed how absent members' projects or unique discoveries should be treated. [C-05, C-11; GDR-SRC-20261010-G15-003, introduction/modifier list; GDR-SRC-20261010-G15-005, §Shared Worlds]

Below Zero’s What the Dock update documents a Custom Game Mode and an Unstuck action. These provide a separate production example of configurable pressure and technical recovery, not proof that removing pressure preserves the same experience. Do not conflate Below Zero with the original Subnautica. [C-05; GDR-SRC-20261010-G15-006, §§Custom Game Mode/Unstuck Button]

Distinguish technical loss from intended consequence. Valheim's specified patch reports corrections involving remote-host saving and persistent world edits. Such reports motivate a separate integrity/recovery review; they do not quantify incident rates or show that a particular player's loss was a bug. Do not defend a failed save as an authored survival lesson. [C-10; GDR-SRC-20261010-G15-007, §Bugs & Issues]

## Practical design choices and application

The following are **conditional proposals**, with no recommended numerical defaults. [C-06–C-14]

| Decision | Use when the intended activity requires it | Compare against / watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Manual gathering versus selective automation | Handling materials or planning repeated trips is itself valued | Project-level purchases, batch actions or automation when execution is no longer the interesting choice; automation can erase a desired hands-on role |
| Fixed recipe versus substitutable inputs | Predictable cost and deliberate dependency knowledge matter | Substitution may support improvisation but complicates explanation and balance; cosmetic variation is not automatically mechanical expression |
| Consumable repair, time-based repair or no ongoing repair cost | Degradation creates understandable preparation or retreat decisions | Irrelevant click maintenance, loss of the only repair route, or repeated punishment for experimenting with unfamiliar tools |
| Carrying/stack constraints | Trip composition, shared hauling or placement is a chosen problem | An expandable project ledger or less friction when storage sorting repeatedly replaces the chosen task; do not diagnose hoarding as irrational without knowing future-use uncertainty |
| Local depletion versus renewal | Geography and finite investment should matter | Renewable alternatives, relocation, salvage or trade when depletion would end a promised continuing activity; unlimited supply can remove intended territorial stakes |
| Recipe disclosure and resource lookup | Recall or reacquisition, rather than first discovery, is the barrier | Notebook, habitat hints or no automated survey; showing every unknown recipe may reveal intended discoveries |
| Shared progression and material ownership | Contributions must coordinate across sessions | Personal goals, recoverable reservations or consent at milestones; an always-online world does not resolve group absence |

**Application sequence.** First write the desired decision in ordinary language: for example, choosing an expedition kit rather than merely increasing production. Next map one complete chain, including the minimum restart route and the authority to use each shared resource. Then identify the actual bottleneck and choose the smallest plausible intervention; preserve an unchanged comparison. Before any separately authorized prototype, write persistence and setting-transition rules. Finally compare comprehension, feasible alternatives, chosen activity and subjective burden, rather than treating throughput as the sole outcome. Stop and revise when a supported state has no honest continuation, when the informational policy cannot be explained, or when the proposed intervention removes the activity people chose. [C-06–C-14]

## Worked examples and counterexamples

### Illustrative worked case: the storm-coast workshop

**Invented design exercise, not a shipped-game observation.** A player wants to survey flooded ruins. A breathing filter can be made from woven fiber and charcoal at a dry bench. Fiber can be gathered by hand; charcoal can be obtained from a simple fire. A metal cutter provides a faster fiber route but is not required for the basic filter. The player may instead salvage a damaged filter, repairing it at the bench. These alternatives are deliberately local example rules, not recommendations for a particular game. [C-07, C-14]

The chain review asks whether loss of the cutter and flooding of the bench prevents continuing. A design might let the player establish a crude dry platform from hand-gatherable materials, preserving a costly but viable restart. It might instead make evacuation the coherent end of that expedition. Either outcome must match the promised activity. A recipe popup cannot fix a world in which neither materials nor exit are reachable. The player-knowledge review separately asks whether the restart relation can be inferred without revealing the ruin's contents. [C-06, C-07]

After finding fiber once, an optional survey can identify known beds within a surveyed coast. It reports yesterday's observation as historical, not guaranteed present stock, and offers a list as well as a map. It does not name a rare fiber species from an unexplored cavern. To test usefulness, compare this with a player notebook while keeping resource supply and travel unchanged. Do not simultaneously double yields and credit the survey for the result. [C-09, C-13, C-14]

### Illustrative social and hybrid countercase

In the same imaginary world, one player enjoys daily hauling while another wants a weekly building session. Automating every material may remove the first player's chosen activity; requiring both players to maintain perishable stock may coerce the second into unwanted scheduling. Separate optional personal projects from essential shared upkeep and investigate whether either arrangement is actually valued. This is a hypothesis, not a validated group-management policy. [C-08, C-11]

Now add a soulslike encounter whose retries require newly crafted filters. Each defeat may trigger both combat practice and a material collection loop. These costs might create a valued expedition decision, or substitute previously solved labor for learning the encounter. Compare retry access, material loss and encounter demand separately. Do not import the entire convention set of both labels without auditing their combined cost. [C-15; linked [soulslike entry](soulslike.md)]

### Strongest contrary case

A deliberately finite, self-contained expedition may promise that supplies eventually run out and not every mistake is recoverable. In that context a renewable fallback, universal locator or free repair can undermine the central problem. The proposed audit should make that commitment legible and test the intended experience, not mandate endless continuation. No empirical evidence here establishes the best compromise between finite stakes and continuing-world expression. [C-07–C-09, C-14]

## Accessibility, ethics, localization and production

For **motor access**, examine repeated gathering inputs, sustained holds, precision picking, rapid inventory transfers and simultaneous movement/menu demands. Compare remapping, toggle/batch alternatives and an explicit paused planning mode where compatible with the session model. A shared world cannot silently pause only for one person while claiming everyone has the same temporal state. These are candidate adaptations requiring evaluation, not a certification checklist. [C-13]

For **sensory and cognitive access**, make ingredient identity, current quantity, reserved quantity, prerequisites and loss consequences available through usable representations. A color difference alone does not communicate every state to every user. A narration route must distinguish item, station and recipe names and remain usable with long inventories. Memory assistance can preserve a learned fact without announcing all undiscovered facts. [C-13]

For **localization**, test ingredient synonyms, plural forms, compound recipe names, search terms, text expansion and unit meaning. Keep internal identity separate from display text so a renamed item is not mistaken for an additional resource. If a chain uses culturally specific tool knowledge, establish how unfamiliar users receive the necessary information rather than assuming a universal craft vocabulary. These are proposed review tasks, not findings about named language groups. [C-13, C-14]

For **ethics**, do not interpret stockpiling, automation, slow play or assistance as character flaws. Investigate whether resource decay and shared maintenance create pressure beyond the intended session commitment. Ecological extraction and cultural representation also deserve review: an endlessly replenishing resource model or a depiction of “empty” land can carry meanings the mechanical analysis has not evaluated. This batch does not establish cultural consensus or ecological learning effects. [C-11–C-14]

For **production**, prioritize recipe/state compatibility, save migration, shared ownership, concurrent consumption, world changes and stale lookup results. Every supported setting combination needs a coherent account of progression; adding options without that account can create unsupported states. Document technical corruption separately from authored attrition. No engine architecture, migration code, capacity estimate or networking solution is authorized here. [C-07, C-10, C-13, C-14]

## Research questions and proposed validation

**All tests below are proposed and NOT PERFORMED.** Recruitment, sample size and decision margins must be justified for the actual question; no numerical default or power claim is supplied. Include differing prior genre knowledge, chosen activities, time constraints and relevant access needs, without using diagnosis as a requirement to request assistance. [C-14]

| Test | Comparison and procedure | Measures, confounds and falsifying decision |
|---|---|---|
| T-SC-1: dependency understanding | Give a supported attempt, then a case where the same material/station rule makes a different response appropriate. Collect a prediction before feedback; retain access cues. | Separate rule explanation, permissible route and preferred choice. Prior recipe knowledge and answer-leading hints confound results. Reject the explanation if success occurs only on repeated surface patterns. |
| T-SC-2: lookup versus reacquisition | Compare notebook and optional discovery-scoped survey after equivalent initial discovery; keep resources, travel and hazards matched. | Record lookup use, wrong-scope inferences, actionable route choices, burden and discovery preference. Novelty/order and spatial memory matter. Reject broad rollout if saved search removes valued exploration or leaks protected facts. |
| T-SC-3: repair and recovery | Compare a relevant consumable-repair rule with a lower-friction alternative in the same provisioning task; include last-tool and depleted-stock cases. | Ask what changed in preparation/retreat decisions and whether restart is feasible and understood. Do not call faster completion better without the chosen goal. Reject a rule that blocks a promised continuing activity with no disclosed exit. |
| T-SC-4: asynchronous group progress | Use an explicitly consented hypothetical or later authorized prototype scenario with an absent contributor, shared stock and a unique milestone. | Evaluate permission comprehension, unwanted obligations, catch-up options and group disagreement, not just server uptime. Familiarity, power imbalance and role preference are confounds. Revise when the arrangement works only through unspoken social pressure. |

# Survival horror

**Complete bounded genre candidate; version 0.1.0 draft. Not released reference knowledge.** This file covers the genre’s design decisions, not the whole history or every game using the label. Read its claims and limits alongside the [G01 review packet](../reviews/full-run-20261010-g01-review-packet.md). All worked scenes and studies proposed here are **illustrative / not performed**. [C-16, C-20]

## Decision and scope

**Decision:** How should a horror-oriented game make survival decisions consequential without confusing scarcity with inconvenience, vulnerability with unusable controls, or fear with repeated punishment?

**Recommendation:** For retry-supported exploration, begin with a comprehensible survival decision: what can be spent, what could be lost, what alternatives remain, and how the player recovers after a mistake. Adjust the *consequence structure* before automatically removing competence or adding deprivation. This is a reasoned design inference with an untested emotional benefit, not a universal survival-horror formula. [C-09–C-11]

Included: tactical survival, resource commitment, navigation and return routes, encounters, progression, recovery, access and genre hybrids. Excluded: engine architecture, quantitative economy balancing, clinical claims, a comprehensive combat topic, and assigning all horror games a single audience. Platform, perspective, multiplayer and retro presentation remain facets. [C-01, C-13, C-20]

## Definition and boundaries

**Working definition — inference, proposed for review:** survival horror foregrounds sustaining a vulnerable character or group through a threatening situation, with meaningful decisions about exposure, limited means, avoidance, confrontation or recovery. Horror is an intended experience; survival is an organizing decision problem. A product’s label is evidence of how it is presented, not proof that all of its modes deliver this same loop. [C-01]

“Resources” can mean health, ammunition, light, tools, safe routes or time *when they constrain actual choices*. Do not redescribe every button press as resource management just to include every horror game. “Vulnerability” concerns possible adverse consequences relative to available responses; it does not require inaccurate input, uncaptioned warnings or a helpless protagonist. “Scarcity” means limited relative to competing uses, not an arbitrary low count. [C-09, C-11, C-13]

| Neighbor or facet | Working boundary for retrieval | Consequence for this file |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Survival / survival crafting | Maintaining life or building capacity need not pursue horror. | Use this genre only where threat interpretation and survival pressure alter decisions; do not import a hunger loop automatically. |
| Action horror | Combat mastery can be central while horror remains intended. | Examine what constrains engagement, rather than using the presence of guns as a classifier. |
| Stealth / pursuit | Avoidance is one survival strategy, not a genre proof. | A linear chase may need timing and recovery guidance more than inventory systems. |
| [Psychological horror](psychological-horror.md) | Interpretation, identity or reality can be the primary unsettled problem. | Both labels can apply; bodily survival and interpretive stakes need not rise together. |
| Retro, fixed camera, first person, VR, cooperative | Presentation, platform or social facets. | No facet is a necessary condition here. Their effects require local evaluation. |

These are proposed routing distinctions, not a formal standard or mutually exclusive taxonomy. [C-01, C-18]

## Custom research instructions

1. **Trace a survival decision, not only a scare.** Record the state before commitment, player-visible information, available alternatives, resources spent, threat response and recoverable state. Separate the designer’s intended scarcity from player-perceived scarcity. [C-09, C-16]
2. **Compare competence fairly.** When comparing armed, unarmed and tool-based play, record aim demands, threat durability, available space, noise consequences and prior learning. “Weapons destroy horror” is not an acceptable starting premise. [C-02, C-11]
3. **Research failure as experienced repetition.** Distinguish being defeated, losing progress, restarting a known sequence, waiting for a retry and discovering that a save is depleted. Record whether a scene becomes mastered, exhausting or narratively interesting on return. [C-05, C-10]
4. **Sample contrasting structures.** Include an inventory-led expedition, a systemic pursuer, a combat-capable case and an optional lower-consequence mode. This corpus offers documented examples of those choices, not a representative sample of players or all regions. [C-02–C-06]
5. **Challenge inherited craft claims.** Seek cases where less scarcity improves engagement, where mastery coexists with fear, where safe navigation supports rather than removes unease, and where a director adds less value than authored encounters. Explicitly search for failed implementations and null results. [C-04, C-11–C-13]
6. **Require build-specific observation before upgrading examples.** Future observation records need observer, date, platform, build, difficulty, assist settings and scene/recording locator. Current creator and editorial accounts are not observations made by this batch. [C-03, C-16]
7. **Recruit on preferences and access needs, not courage.** Separate desired threat intensity, desired tactical challenge, familiarity, reading demands and sensory/motor access. Never infer a player’s fear tolerance from disability or skill. [C-07, C-14, C-16]

## Player promises and varied audiences

Treat these as **candidate audience goals**, not universal expectations: surviving through prudent decisions; mastering an initially threatening system; investigating a place despite danger; experiencing vulnerability without long retries; or understanding the story while reducing confrontation. Published alternative modes and differing creator preferences justify considering this range, but provide no prevalence estimates. [C-05–C-07, C-16]

Ask a prospective audience what should remain difficult. A player may want exacting resource decisions but easier aiming, or intense themes with negligible lost progress. A broad “easy” setting may change unwanted variables. Conversely, removing every cost may remove the very planning activity another player sought. Define a mode in terms of its changed consequences, not as “the authentic experience” versus an inferior substitute. [C-07, C-19]

## Characteristic loops and structures

The following are analytical structures to test, not descriptions of every genre member. [C-01, C-16]

**Moment-to-moment:** perceive a relevant threat or constraint → assess an approach → commit an action or resource → observe a consequence → revise. A choice to withdraw can be meaningful; immobility caused by an unreadable interface is not the same thing.

**Expedition/session:** select an objective and loadout → leave a planning location → resolve hazards and route changes → obtain information or means → return, establish a new recovery position, or accept a retry. Inventory capacity creates a different decision from item rarity: one constrains what can be carried; the other constrains what exists.

**Longer progression:** route knowledge, new tools and improved skill change which risks can be managed. Decide whether that growing competence is a reward or a reason to introduce genuinely new problems. Repeatedly invalidating mastered rules can make learning pointless. A mostly linear pursuer game may instead progress through changing situations without a large economy. [C-02, C-09, C-11]

### Mechanisms and evidence-to-application bridge

| Candidate causal link | What supports it here | What remains unestablished |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Limited means plus competing uses → opportunity cost | Decision logic and documented tool/inventory precedents. [C-02, C-03, C-09] | That players perceive the tradeoff, or that small numbers alone create it. |
| Legible alternatives → a credible survival decision | Developer descriptions and reasoning about action consequences. [C-09, C-10] | The best number of options, information precision or control scheme. |
| Consequential choice → perceived vulnerability → anticipation | Practitioner rationales motivate the hypothesis. [C-11] | Direct causal comparison; frustration, cognitive effort and novelty are alternatives. |
| Recovery opportunities → ability to continue the agreed activity | Access guidance and a bounded design obligation. [C-07, C-10] | Equal fear, better enjoyment, or one ideal checkpoint spacing. |
| Environmental treatment → fear | Narrow adjacent original-study findings, including nulls. [C-12] | Genre universals, safe sensory settings, or fear during resource decisions. |

Do not multiply these links into a single “validated system.” A coherent economy can be frightening, satisfying, tedious or irrelevant depending on the chosen activity and audience. [C-11, C-16]

## Practical choices and alternatives

| Decision | Candidate choice and reason | Alternatives and exit condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scarcity | Limit a resource shared by meaningful uses. | Use abundant tools with positional risk when deprivation only produces hoarding or repeated errands. |
| Confrontation | Permit costly combat, avoidance or environmental responses. | Remove combat when authoring it adds irrelevant mastery; do not remove all agency with it. |
| Exploration | Make route knowledge change the price or exposure of returning. | Prefer a linear structure when backtracking offers no new decision. |
| Inventory | Use capacity when carrying something means leaving another useful option. | Simplify inventory when repeated sorting, not survival judgment, dominates the session. |
| Failure | Declare loss, retry and recovery behavior. | Lower rollback, provide an assist or separate mode; retain optional costly failure when it is the chosen promise. |
| Encounter pacing | Compare authored sequences with constrained dynamic variation. | Do not build a director simply to match another studio’s feature list. |
| Progression | Expand solutions or competence in ways that remain useful. | Accept mastery instead of automatically restoring fear through hidden rule changes. |

These are conditional proposals derived from C-02–C-11 and C-15, not measured rankings.

### Application process

**Diagnose:** write the target decision and the unwanted observed problem that a later prototype must test. “Not scary enough” is too coarse: identify whether the problem is effortless domination, no credible alternative, illegible danger, repetitive rollback, or a player who prefers another activity.

**Map consequences:** sketch the resource sources/uses and routes on paper. Mark each essential gate, depletion state, recoverable mistake and explicit terminal failure. Distinguish a costly but viable route from a hidden dead end. No economy simulation or engine work was done here.

**Choose a simpler comparison:** change one decision relationship before adding content. Compare resource pressure with a less restrictive version, or a penalty-heavy retry with a shorter rollback; keep fiction and immediate threat behavior comparable where possible.

**Specify communication and exceptions:** define what can be inferred, what must be known to act, what remains uncertain and how assistance represents the same required facts. Specify whether a refuge is mechanically safe, merely quieter, or explicitly temporary. A protected restorative interval must not be secretly reclassified as an ambush for the sake of surprise.

**Evaluate and narrow:** retain a cost only when it contributes to the agreed activity. A lack of additional fear is not automatically failure if the intended benefit is clearer planning; conversely, higher fear cannot excuse an inaccessible critical cue. [C-09–C-11, C-14–C-16, C-18]

## Pattern selection and adaptations

**Existing contextual audio anomaly:** use only as an optional draft hypothesis when the player can recognize a relevant context and investigate or respond. A resource-critical warning must not become ambiguous merely to serve the pattern. A caption should not reveal more than the meaningful cue, but necessary information cannot be withheld from players who rely on it. This is a bounded adaptation, not proof that the recipe adds anticipation. [C-08, C-17]

**New [Configurable threat consequences](../patterns/configurable-threat-consequences.md), GDR-CAND-20261010-G01-PAT-01:** consider when the audience wants the setting or investigation but not a particular loss/retry barrier. It separates threat presentation, harmful consequences and progression recovery. It does **not** guarantee the same survival loop or emotional experience. Prefer smaller targeted adjustments when those suffice. Proposed general ownership requires Foundations and Systems coordination. [C-19]

**Hybrid conflicts:** for a scene combining psychological interpretation with survival pressure, decide whether being chased is meant to interrupt reading, and offer a later recoverable evidence route if it does. For undisturbed restorative exploration, suspend active punishment during the promised protected interval or move threat uncertainty outside it. A “cozy” tag does not make unwanted fear acceptable, and a “horror” tag does not cancel an explicit refuge promise. [C-18]

**Source-derived cases; not game observations performed here.** The RE2 guide’s linked inventory and route advice illustrates a planning-oriented case. Frictional’s Bunker discussion supplies a tools-and-systemic-solutions case; its older account supports a contrasting reduction of combat. Neither establishes a causal ranking. [C-02, C-03]

**Important negative evidence:** some seemingly horror-compatible environmental treatments were not distinguishable from a neutral comparison in the inspected study. The study’s rooms, samples and outcomes cannot decide whether a whole game is frightening. Do not replace this uncertainty with “darkness always works” or “fog never works.” Exact methods and values are confined to C-12. [C-12, C-13]

### Illustrative worked example — archive evacuation route

**Synthetic design exercise; no assets, prototype, game canon or player data.** A player must retrieve an evacuation roster from an archive while a roaming threat makes some routes risky. A portable battery can operate a quiet door release or support a scanning tool. A manual crank can also open the door, but exposes the player for longer. A longer maintenance passage avoids spending the battery yet requires navigating an uncertain patrol crossing. [C-16]

The intended survival decision is not “guess which door the designer wanted.” It is to choose between expenditure, exposure and route uncertainty. Before commitment, the player can identify the destination, inspect the door’s power requirement and learn that the crank is usable. Exact threat position may remain unknown. The alternative-modality presentation conveys relevant patrol events; it does not label the threat’s intentions as certain.

The paper-state audit asks: can the player spend the battery elsewhere, lose health, abandon a carried object, retreat or reload? When depleted, the maintenance route remains possible under the stated retry-supported mode, or the player can knowingly restart from the planning point. The game need not guarantee success at every action. It should not conceal that an old save has become incapable of progressing.

A comparison version removes the battery’s competing use but retains the same route geometry, patrol and objective. If players report identical anticipation and make equivalent decisions, the economy may add accounting rather than meaningful pressure. Another version preserves cost but communicates the alternatives clearly; improved choices there would implicate information design, not insufficient scarcity. All comparisons are proposed. [C-09–C-11, C-16]

### Illustrative counterexample — compulsory precision chase

A short chase has one route, no strategic resource use and an essential turn signaled only by sound. Adding rare healing items and a distant checkpoint does not create a new survival decision; it mostly changes the price of missing an inaccessible cue. First fix information access and response demands, or explicitly redefine the scene’s purpose. This counterexample challenges the recommendation to add scarcity indiscriminately; it does not prove that linear chases cannot be effective horror. [C-08–C-11, C-16]

## Accessibility, ethics, localization and production

**Access:** test captioned critical events, legible route states, selectable controls, pause/reading behavior and reduced-motion presentation independently from resource challenge. A low-resource mode is not a substitute for access to the interface. Lowering threat lethality does not make upsetting themes comfortable for every player. No photosensitivity compliance, safe sound level, medical result or disability simulation is claimed. [C-07, C-08, C-14, C-19]

**Ethics and localization — proposed review obligations:** disclose relevant disturbing content and allow withdrawal; do not secretly punish someone for choosing assistance. Review whether threatened bodies, hostile institutions or fictional labels use real disability, religion or marginalized identity as an unexamined danger signal. Record intended cultural meaning rather than borrowing a “universal creepy” prop list. Red Candle’s situated creator framing is a reminder to research particular contexts, not a license to generalize a culture. No stigma effect or cultural reception was measured. [C-14, C-16]

**Production:** track route/gate combinations, depleted inventories, encounter triggers, refuge exceptions, save versions and each supported assist mode. More combinations mean more things whose consistency must be checked; this is a work-category inference, not an exponential cost estimate. Keep a reproducible content-state record before considering sophisticated dynamic orchestration. No harness, engine, middleware or settings change is authorized. [C-15, C-20]

## Research questions and validation

**Status: all proposed; no tests performed.** The main unresolved question is whether credible alternatives under a chosen constraint produce valued anticipation rather than merely effort. Secondary questions concern recovery, repeat exposure and access. [C-11, C-16]

**Proposed local formative comparison:** 12 consenting adults, six relatively unfamiliar and six practiced in comparable survival play; within each familiarity group allocate three to each A/B order. This is a small workload/order-coverage proposal, not a power calculation or a population claim. Include actual relevant access needs at participants’ preferred settings; absence of relevant access evaluation blocks an accessibility conclusion. [C-16]

A uses the archive’s competing battery uses; B removes that competition while keeping the objective, threat behavior and route layout as similar as feasible. Use two matched layouts to limit memory transfer, rotate mapping and log unavoidable differences. Record first exposure and a later repeat separately. Freezing the exact protocol and approving recruitment require a later authorized study owner.

Collect separately: articulated options and costs; anticipation, fear, frustration and perceived agency; actual route/spend choice and reason; essential-fact comprehension; retries and replay time; comfort and voluntary continuation. Locally worded 0–6 ratings would be exploratory instruments, not validated scales. Behavior or arousal alone is not a fear measure.

**Proposed decision rule:** advance only to further testing if the constraint produces at least one additional correctly articulated consequential tradeoff in the median paired comparison, with a consistent directional anticipation advantage in both order groups, and no critical information-access or progression failure. These are provisional formative thresholds, not evidence of efficacy. A workload rise without a new understood choice favors simplification. A benefit only for first exposure narrows the use case. An inconclusive sample is not equivalence.

**Falsifying cases:** participants cannot identify competing uses; the supposedly optional route dominates every rational choice; the effect disappears when warning legibility is matched; or a chosen access setting removes an essential fact. Any distress or request to stop ends that session without penalty. Do not delete withdrawal as an inconvenient outlier. [C-09–C-11, C-14, C-16]

**Separate future questions, not extra studies secretly run:** compare rollback policies while keeping encounter challenge fixed; compare authored and dynamic pacing at similar encounter exposure; examine repeat play and more varied input/display contexts. No sample size or numerical success standard transfers from the supplied audio pilot. [C-04, C-05, C-15–C-17]

# Psychological horror

**Complete bounded genre candidate; version 0.1.0 draft. Not released reference knowledge.** This file owns interpretive and character-centered horror decisions, not a clinical definition or a survey of all works carrying the label. Its [paired survival-horror entry](survival-horror.md) owns the survival-oriented comparison. All illustrative scenes and proposed tests are **not performed**. [C-01, C-16, C-20]

## Decision and scope

**Decision:** How can a game make what the player believes, remembers, understands or feels responsible for disturbingly consequential, without substituting arbitrary confusion, psychiatric stereotypes or inaccessible presentation for that experience?

**Recommendation:** Specify the troubling proposition and the evidence a player can use to interpret it. Preserve usable access to the intended actions while allowing meaning, causation or identity to remain unsettled where that uncertainty serves the scene. The recommendation is a conditional inference; the claim that coherent ambiguity adds more unease is a **low-confidence hypothesis**, not demonstrated genre knowledge. [C-08, C-09, C-19]

Included: interpretive stakes, character perspective, contradictory evidence, spatial/narrative revision, symbolic specificity, encounter alternatives, accessibility, localization and representation. Excluded: diagnosing players or characters, claiming therapeutic benefit, a full mental-health evidence review, engine implementation and a universal recipe for frightening an audience. [C-01, C-12, C-20]

## Definition and boundaries

**Working definition — inference, proposed for review:** psychological horror organizes its disturbing experience around troubling interpretations of self, relationships, agency, knowledge or reality. Bodily danger can coexist with this focus, but the scene’s important question is not exhausted by “can I survive this enemy?” A disclosed outcome may still leave disturbing questions about responsibility, trust or meaning. [C-01, C-10]

The label is **not synonymous with mental illness**, hallucinations, a “sanity” meter, an unreliable protagonist, a surprise ending, photorealism, or the absence of combat. An ordinary social relationship, an intelligible moral predicament or an apparently dependable place can supply interpretive stakes without any clinical framing. Those possibilities are design hypotheses, not automatic effects. [C-01, C-09, C-10, C-12]

| Neighbor or term | Boundary to preserve | Routing implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Survival horror | Consequential survival decisions can coexist with interpretive threat. | Read both entries when an encounter affects resources and the meaning of events. |
| Mystery / detective | Resolving facts or attribution need not be frightening. | Do not turn incomplete information alone into psychological horror. |
| Supernatural / cosmic horror | These describe kinds of fictional explanation or threat. | They can overlap; neither uncertainty nor cosmic scale proves psychological focus. |
| Narrative adventure / exploration | Story-led investigation can aim at curiosity, grief, comfort or wonder instead. | “No combat” is insufficient for genre classification. |
| Mental-health portrayal | A represented experience is not itself a monster, diagnostic tool or genre criterion. | Research the portrayal separately; do not assume fear or harm from the diagnosis. |
| Unreliable narration | One technique for presenting evidence from a limited or mistaken viewpoint. | A reliable narrator or known outcome can still be a candidate here. |

These boundaries describe this candidate’s use, not a universally accepted taxonomy. No neighboring genre file is silently created by the table. [C-01, C-10, C-12, C-15]

### Terms that must not collapse into one another

**Ambiguity** means evidence admits more than one plausible reading. **Uncertainty** means a fact or outcome is unresolved. **Contradiction** means particular claims cannot both hold under the same interpretation; different times or viewpoints may dissolve it. **Confusion** concerns trouble understanding the situation or required action. **Unreliability** describes a source of evidence, not permission for the software to misrepresent real settings or save behavior. These are proposed analytic conventions. [C-08, C-16]

A player can understand what to do while disagreeing about what it means. Equally, a game can deliberately make a fictional action’s effects mysterious without hiding how to access captions or exit. “Usable action information” is not a promise to reveal every probability, future consequence or authorial truth. This qualification is essential to the strongest recommendation. [C-07, C-08, C-11]

## Custom research instructions

1. **Name the feared proposition before selecting an effect.** Is the concern loss of trust, compromised identity, responsibility for harm, unreliable evidence, or something else? Record the proposed emotional target separately from surprise, disgust, curiosity and task confusion. [C-01, C-09, C-16]
2. **Track who knows what.** Distinguish player-accessible evidence, character belief, claims by other characters, and facts deliberately left unresolved. Do not make an unexpected reading “wrong” merely because the author did not anticipate it. Ask whether it is supported by the presented evidence. [C-08, C-09]
3. **Audit contradictions across time and access modes.** For each change, record what changed, what stayed stable, who could notice, which interpretation it supports, and whether a caption, map, journal or translation accidentally discloses or conceals an extra fact. [C-08, C-11, C-13]
4. **Compare alternatives to perpetual ambiguity.** Test a known but troubling outcome, overtly supernatural framing, character testimony, or no additional distortion. Do not assume an unresolved ending is more mature, frightening or engaging. [C-09, C-10]
5. **Challenge the helplessness shortcut.** Inspect whether fighting, acting competently or resolving a puzzle can complicate meaning rather than destroy it. Distinguish physical agency from control over the story’s outcome. [C-02, C-15]
6. **Research situated meaning.** Use creator accounts and appropriate cultural/language contributors for particular symbols, family roles, institutions and historical framing. Do not assemble a generic “Asian horror,” “madness” or “religious fear” checklist. [C-03–C-05, C-12]
7. **Treat representation as a separate review responsibility.** Record consultation scope, disagreements and changes. The existence of a consultant, clinical adviser or favorable fan account does not establish accuracy or absence of harm. [C-05, C-12]
8. **Test first and informed encounters separately.** A twist’s surprise and the value of revisiting an already known story are different decisions. Do not generalize from public reactions, edited streams or a famous title’s reputation. [C-14, C-16, C-19]

## Player promises and varied audiences

Potential audience goals include working through disturbing evidence, feeling implicated in a character’s predicament, experiencing an atmospheric story without execution pressure, or revisiting a known story for a different reading. These are hypotheses for recruitment and questioning; this batch has no survey of their prevalence. [C-06, C-14]

Ask separately about tolerance for uncertainty, confrontation, upsetting subject matter, startle and lost progress. A player who wants an unsettling interpretation may still need predictable controls, clear captions and an interruption-safe session. Another may prefer a concrete explanation to open-ended ambiguity. Assistance cannot be assumed to make sensitive themes welcome. [C-06, C-11, C-14, C-18]

## Characteristic loops and structures

**Interpretive loop — proposed:** encounter a detail → relate it to a concern or prior belief → form or revise a reading → choose whether and how to investigate or act → encounter a consequence or new perspective. Each cycle should do more than restate “something is wrong.” It can deepen a supported reading, challenge it, make it consequential, or provide relief. [C-09, C-16]

**Scene/session structure:** establish the immediate activity and stakes; introduce evidence at a pace that leaves room to notice; allow an interpretable response; then consolidate what the player has learned without requiring a single emotional reaction. Interruptions and optional exploration change which evidence has been encountered, so a later scene must not silently assume exhaustive reading. [C-08, C-13, C-16]

**Longer structure:** a later revelation can reinterpret an earlier event, but the earlier event should remain describable. A conclusion may resolve facts while leaving moral or emotional meaning unsettled. Alternatively, uncertainty may persist by design. Neither ending style is validated as superior in this corpus. [C-09, C-10, C-14]

## Practical choices, alternatives and conditional application

### 1. Choose the kind of unresolved question

Specify whether the player is uncertain about events, another character’s account, the protagonist’s interpretation, or the significance of known facts. Keep these choices distinguishable. Withholding a chronology requires a different evidence plan from presenting a clear chronology that has distressing implications. [C-08–C-10]

**Alternative:** disclose the likely outcome and let the difficult question become how to respond. **Exit:** when ambiguity only prevents the player from knowing what is actionable, clarify the task or choose another structure. Do not keep adding unexplained imagery to compensate. [C-08, C-10]

### 2. Specify evidence and degrees of explanation

For each important evidence item, write its literal content, source, possible readings, later reinterpretations and available representations. Identify facts that must survive every permitted route and optional material that may deepen a reading. Give later scenes a recovery route for essential missed evidence rather than assuming every player explored everything. This is a proposed authoring process, not a new stable pattern. [C-08, C-09, C-13]

**Alternative:** use one clear account and let the consequence carry the horror. **Exit:** if the only supported reading depends on knowing an unintroduced cultural reference or hearing an uncaptioned phrase, revise the evidence or narrow the audience claim; do not blame players for failing to infer it. [C-11, C-12]

### 3. Decide what may change and what remains dependable

A room can change layout, an account can contradict a prior account, or an item can acquire a new meaning. These are different manipulations. Mark their triggers and the player’s means of checking what changed. Preserve genuine non-fictional controls and system notices as reliable. A fictional unreliable menu must be clearly bounded as fiction and must not falsify actual data-loss, purchase, privacy or access behavior. This draft authorizes none of those implementation actions. [C-08, C-13, C-16, C-20]

**Alternative:** keep the place stable and change testimony or interpretation. **Exit:** when navigation failure overwhelms the intended reading, reduce spatial changes or supply a usable record of what was experienced. Do not require a memory impairment or visual distortion setting as the price of receiving the story. [C-08, C-11]

### 4. Align action with interpretation

Decide whether combat, concealment, conversation or examination expresses the intended problem, distracts from it, or supplies a deliberate contrast. Avoid inventing a combat ban. A successful action can offer temporary competence while leaving its meaning disturbing; that remains an option to test, not a demonstrated effect. [C-02, C-09, C-15]

**Alternative:** reduce or separate encounters when the target audience cannot attend to both reading and threat. **Exit:** when every interruption makes essential evidence inaccessible, change timing, make evidence recoverable, or reconsider which activity governs the scene. [C-11, C-15]

### 5. Evaluate interpretation, affect and trust separately

Check whether players can justify their reading from available evidence, state the current task, describe what remains uncertain and choose a plausible response. Then ask about unease, fear, curiosity, frustration, comfort and willingness to continue. A coherent interpretation is not evidence of fear; a frightened player is not evidence of coherent interpretation. [C-09, C-16, C-19]

## Pattern selection, adaptations and hybrids

The supplied **Contextual audio anomaly, GDK-PAT-0001, draft 0.1.0** can pose a question about a place or testimony, but does not provide a universal psychological-horror mechanism. A familiar relationship may be established without adding another sound; an overt event, an absence, or no extra device may be preferable. Do not repeat its full recipe here. [C-17]

The new [Configurable threat consequences](../patterns/configurable-threat-consequences.md), **GDR-CAND-20261010-G01-PAT-01**, is relevant when a loss/retry requirement blocks the interpretive activity the player wants. Reducing lethality does not remove theme-related distress, and it can change the activity enough to become a different mode rather than a difficulty variant. [C-06, C-18]

No second general pattern is extracted for “coherent ambiguity.” This corpus motivates a genre-specific authoring proposal but does not establish a distinct cross-genre recipe beyond existing information/narrative responsibilities. Repetition of the same hypothesis under another filename would add no evidence. [C-09, C-19]

For **survival + psychological horror**, decide whether evidence can be read under pressure, deferred safely or retrieved again; explain how resource consequences affect interpretation. For **detective + psychological horror**, declare whether solving facts is promised or whether several readings remain legitimate. For **restorative exploration + psychological horror**, an interval promised as undisturbed cannot simultaneously deliver an unannounced unavoidable threat without renegotiating that promise. Partition scenes or modes rather than claiming one label outranks the other. [C-15]

## Representative and contrary cases

**Creator account:** the Silent Hill 2 remake article motivates a combat-capable boundary, not a rule that combat strengthens or weakens psychological horror. [C-02]

**Directly inspected artifacts, not played examples:** Devotion and Detention provide situated creator framing rather than evidence that a culture, religion or household setting has a uniform psychological effect. Their comparison highlights why a borrowed visual motif is not a substitute for a researched relation between place, characters and meaning. [C-03, C-04, C-12]

**Process boundary:** Hellblade II’s reported consultation is relevant when choosing to represent mental-health experiences; it is not evidence that such a representation defines psychological horror, or that consultation guarantees a harmless or clinically accurate portrayal. [C-05, C-12]

**Strong opposing craft case:** Grip’s argument for vaguer systems conflicts with an absolute requirement that the player understand every rule. The revised recommendation here protects usable information without requiring complete mechanical knowledge. That compromise remains testable and may not fit every intentionally opaque work. [C-07, C-08]

**Negative-evidence limit:** no controlled study directly supporting the main psychological-horror recommendation was inspected. ### Illustrative worked example — a public memorial archive

**Synthetic scene; not a game observation or approved canon.** A clerk must prepare a memorial record. One testimony describes a person as a willing volunteer; a dated inventory suggests the person had already left the institution. The immediate task is clear: inspect the available records and decide which statement to include or mark unresolved. The feared proposition is that an apparently benevolent institution may have erased responsibility. [C-16]

The evidence plan records the exact assertion in each item, its date and author, and which uncertainty remains. One reading is that the records concern different events; another is that the account is self-protective. Neither is declared correct simply because it is more sinister. Later evidence can narrow these readings, but must not erase a previously available fact without representing that change.

The player may inspect a cabinet, request another account, submit a provisional record, or pause. A spatial variant reuses the same office while changing its commemorative labels. The clue is the attribution and date, not an unannounced remapping of the exit. An accessible document view preserves the content at readable size; narration and captions are checked for accidental disambiguation of names and speakers. A localized version needs the same evidentiary relationship, not a word-for-word rendering that changes who is implied to be responsible.

A comparison version explains the discrepancy immediately but retains the same action and emotional stakes. It might create less uncertainty yet more discomfort about knowingly endorsing the record. That is a serious alternative, not a deliberately weak control. If the ambiguous version only causes players to miss the task, its intended psychological mechanism has not succeeded. If the explicit version supports a more meaningful response, prefer it for that purpose. [C-08–C-13, C-16]

### Illustrative counterexample — arbitrary reset masquerading as depth

Every inspected document changes its date unpredictably, the required interaction is unmarked, and a final reveal dismisses all prior evidence as meaningless. This may create surprise or uncertainty, but the proposed evidence-based interpretive loop has failed: the player has nothing stable with which to reason. The repair need not explain the whole story. Preserve a describable observation, assign a reason or bounded rule to a change, or admit that the work aims at a different experience. No player result is claimed. [C-08, C-09, C-16]

### Illustrative alternative — known outcome, unresolved responsibility

Disclose that a building will be demolished. Let the player choose whose testimony to preserve, with the demolition no longer a mystery. The candidate unease concerns participation and responsibility, not whether a monster will appear or whether the environment is real. This is an alternative hypothesis that challenges treating ambiguity as necessary. It has not been tested. [C-10, C-16]

## Accessibility, ethics, localization and production

**Information and access:** caption significant speech and relevant sounds; check speaker attribution, remembered phrases, simultaneous voices and document presentation. An assistive description can accidentally name a source the ordinary presentation leaves unknown, while a vague label can conceal information needed to act. Evaluate the actual required facts and interpretive opportunities with people using those presentations; do not promise identical emotion. [C-08, C-11]

**Sensory presentation:** reduced-motion, readable and pausable forms of a sequence are design options to review, not admissions that horror has been removed. A changing identity, testimony or place need not be conveyed through forced camera shaking. No safe flashing threshold, clinical recommendation or tested configuration is supplied here; assessment of hazardous sensory content is an uncompleted specialist dependency. [C-11, C-16]

**Representation and research ethics — proposed obligations:** make sensitive-content information available before participation, separate spoiler-sensitive descriptions from actionable content notices, allow adjustment and withdrawal, and avoid recruiting people on the assumption that a diagnosis makes them suitable fear-test subjects. Fictional distress is not permission to mislead participants about real privacy, data loss or consent. No treatment, desensitization or stigma-reduction claim is made. [C-05, C-12, C-16]

**Localization:** provide translators with speaker knowledge, viewpoint, date ordering, ambiguity that must survive and facts that must not become uncertain. Family address forms, grammatical gender, tense, honorifics, names and reading order can create design questions even when the source sentence is short; these are proposed audit categories, not findings from localized builds. Review culturally situated references with suitable contributors rather than replacing them with generic ominous imagery. [C-11–C-13, C-16]

**Production:** maintain an evidence/state table for each meaningful variant, including trigger, route, prior knowledge, access representation, translation context, save/reload and optional content. A reused room can still require considerable narrative and verification work. Prefer fewer meaningful changes over many variants that cannot be reviewed coherently; the tradeoff is unmeasured and project-dependent. [C-13, C-16]

| Future warning sign | Competing interpretation | Proposed response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Players cannot identify the next possible action. | Operational confusion rather than desired interpretive uncertainty. | Clarify interaction and reassess before adding atmosphere. |
| Players infer a revelation only from captions or translated grammar. | Unequal information, not merely a different emotional response. | Audit semantics and disclosure timing across presentations. |
| Every new fact makes earlier evidence irrelevant. | Arbitrary contradiction or excessive revision. | Preserve inspectable evidence and a supported relation between changes. |
| Players understand the scene but find it uninteresting. | Weak stakes, known convention or irrelevant theme. | Change the proposition or accept another audience; obscurity is not the automatic repair. |

| Reduced penalties still leave the experience unwanted. | Theme or sensory intensity, not failed execution, is the barrier. | Respect exit or choose different content; do not promise that a “safe” mode suits everyone. |

## Research questions and validation

**PROPOSED / NOT PERFORMED.** The primary question is whether supported, unresolved interpretation adds valued unease while preserving access to the immediate activity. The alternative is not “all explanation is bad”; a known outcome may be preferable. [C-09, C-10, C-16]

**Local formative design proposal:** 16 consenting adults, eight per version, with each version including four relatively unfamiliar and four practiced readers/players of comparable narrative horror. This is a workload and variation-coverage proposal, not a power calculation or a prevalence estimate. Include people who actually use the relevant reading/caption/access options; no artificial simulation of disability. Recruitment and any prototype require separate approval. [C-16]

Version A leaves the memorial discrepancy unresolved but makes the available actions clear. Version B explains the discrepancy early while retaining the choice of what to preserve. Use parallel evidence materials and equivalent reading opportunity where feasible; document differences in information and narrative emphasis. This is a comparison of authoring strategies, **not a pure ambiguity manipulation**: revealing an explanation can change the story’s meaning. Do not attribute every effect to uncertainty alone.

Do not show the same participant both explanations of the same story and pretend the second encounter is naïve. Use separate groups for first exposure and label a later informed repeat as a different question. Record prior plot knowledge, cultural familiarity, literacy/language context, chosen presentation and interruptions. [C-14, C-16]

Before data collection, define essential task facts, evidence-supported readings and what would count as a new but defensible reading. Ask participants to identify their evidence and explain the available actions. Assess ongoing unease, anticipation, curiosity, confusion, frustration, comfort, agency and voluntary continuation separately. A locally worded 0–6 rating scale is only an exploratory proposal; no validated new instrument is asserted.

**Proposed local advancement rule:** at least six of the eight A participants can state the immediate task and justify a reading from accessible evidence; A’s median ongoing-unease rating exceeds B’s by at least one point without a worse median task-understanding rating or a critical access failure. This is a formative design heuristic, not statistical proof or an approved threshold. Report all responses and departures, not only medians. A novel supported reading is not automatically an error.

**Reject or narrow:** reject the coherent-ambiguity explanation when the apparent benefit is only immediate surprise, increased reading workload, missing instructions or unequal evidence. Prefer the explicit strategy if it better supports the intended interpretation without an unacceptable experiential cost. A first-exposure-only effect narrows applicability. A small null result is inconclusive, not proof of equivalence. Any distress or withdrawal request ends the session without penalty. [C-08–C-11, C-14, C-16, C-19]

**Separate future review:** use culturally and clinically appropriate contributors when relevant to the chosen portrayal; record disagreements and changes instead of asking them to certify an entire genre. This is not an independent review already performed. [C-05, C-12, C-16]

# Party

**Draft research candidate, not released knowledge.** Scope: G19 only. Definitions and application recipes are reversible research proposals, not approved studio decisions. No game implementation, participant test, independent review or publication occurred in this batch.

## Definition and boundaries

A **party game**, as used here, deliberately supports a shared group occasion through legible participation opportunities, interpretable rounds or focal events, and handling of varied familiarity or contribution. “Short” is relative to the group’s occasion and tolerance for waiting, not a universal duration. Multiplayer alone, being funny, a small control set or a game played at a social gathering does not settle the classification. This is a working design boundary, not a market-category verdict. [P-01]

Party play can be competitive, cooperative, asymmetric, performative, physical, verbal or text-based. The inspected examples establish a narrower range: prompt/vote play and asymmetric cooperative communication, with audience and moderation documentation. Physical games, large public events, children’s groups and sustained remote communities remain insufficiently examined. An established specialist game played by friends may offer excellent social value without being designed for repeated onboarding or varied roles. Do not relabel it simply because its players laugh. [P-01, P-09]

[Social deduction](social-deduction.md) is separate: a party can involve no adversarial information manipulation. [Rhythm](rhythm.md) is also separate: a musical performance round has temporal and motor constraints not answered by party-session design. Group size, local/online mode, platform and tone are facets to record; no universal “ideal party size” is justified here. [P-06, P-13]

## Custom research instructions

Begin with the **group context**: acquaintances or strangers, recurring or one-off gathering, shared room or remote, available devices, language access, facilitation capacity and the ability to decline participation. Record what people can actually do before asking whether a game feels social. Distinguish authoring an answer, voting, negotiating, watching, facilitating and resting; label any change in influence between these roles. [P-09, P-12]

Inspect full round rules, examples of explanations, audience permissions, settings, recovery behavior and re-entry state. Compare a participant waiting voluntarily with a person denied a contribution route. Seek a quieter satisfied player and an expressive excluded player as contrary cases to behavioral shorthand; do not diagnose personality from either. A host’s belief that everyone understood is not a substitute for participant evidence. [P-07, P-09]

Include groups with mixed familiarity, different communication needs and unequal access to devices. Do not assume speech is available, all participants share humor references, or every audience member wants to become a performer. Inspect source dates per feature: live publisher tables can contain later additions under an older article date. Storefront capacity or duration is a product claim, not a genre formula. [P-03–P-05, P-10]

The next source routes should include complete rules and observed versioned sessions, communication-access studies, practitioner production accounts that discuss failures, and participant-defined outcomes. The laughter paper 022 remains abstract-only and supplies no results here. Neither commercial success nor a montage of reactions validates inclusion. [P-08, P-09]

## Player promises and varied audiences

Candidate promises are **having a way to contribute, understanding why a round resolved as it did, sharing an event worth noticing, and being able to recover or stop without humiliation**. These are proposals for research, not universal expectations or approved studio policies. Some groups seek vigorous competition; others value performance, conversation, discovery or low-pressure observation. Equal button presses need not mean equal influence, and equal influence need not be everyone’s preferred role. [P-01, P-09, P-10]

The critical distinction is between an available role and a valued, accessible, freely chosen role. A voter may be central to one person’s enjoyment and a frustrating substitute for another person’s wish to submit work. Keep those possibilities open. Do not turn “spectators can interact” into “spectators belong,” or turn an invitation to play into a requirement to disclose an access need to the whole group. [P-03, P-09, P-12]

## Characteristic loops and session structure

A useful party-session analysis is **join/learn → choose or receive a role → contribute → resolve publicly understandable consequences → recover, rotate, repeat or leave**. This is a synthesis of the examined structures, not a required genre loop. Separate the clocks for explanation, active contribution, waiting, result display and re-entry. A brief active round can still create a long period of involuntary waiting. [P-01, P-12]

At the round level, decisions include whether everyone acts simultaneously, takes turns, contributes different resources, or alternates between producing and judging. At the session level, decide how newcomers learn the current rule and whether joining changes scoring or private knowledge. Long-term progression is optional; when used, its advantage can conflict with a newcomer’s immediate contribution. That conflict needs an explicit local choice rather than an assumption that persistent progression is always motivating. [P-10–P-13]

## Mechanisms and practical design choices

### Explain the action and consequence without assuming the group learned

A concise demonstration can expose what action is expected and what follows, but successful imitation may conceal an unlearned rule. Apply the corrected F01 distinction: present new cases where the **same** rule requires different correct predictions. In a vote-based round, ask what happens in both a tied and untied illustrative result under the announced tie rule; do not repeatedly ask whether the highest count wins and call identical answers transfer. Keep tie rules local to the prototype, not attributed to a shipped title. [P-11]

Separate rule prediction from answer quality, improvisational fluency and interface execution. Someone who understands how to submit may still need more time to compose; someone who has a clever response may not know whether a button sends it publicly. Persistent reminders can remain without turning the activity into a tutorial forever. A practice round is optional when discovery itself is the chosen pleasure, but consequential uncertainty should then be intentional rather than an unnoticed explanation failure. [P-11, P-12]

### Give different roles explicit privileges and transitions

Jackbox’s audience documentation distinguishes capabilities by title; its rotation suggestion is practitioner advice. This supports asking what a role permits, not a conclusion that rotation improves belonging. [P-03, P-04; GDR-SRC-20261010-G19-008]

The proposed application is a **participation map**: list each role’s visible information, allowed action, decision influence, waiting interval, exit and next entry point. Then ask participants which role they want and whether it is accessible. Offer rather than impose rotation. When a role is unavailable, say so plainly; a substitute audience seat should not be represented as an equivalent authoring seat. A simpler alternative is a fixed cooperative role set chosen before play, or an unscored shared activity without seat competition. [P-10, P-12]

This approach has costs. Rotation can require another explanation, interrupt concentration, change scoring meaning and expose hidden information. Optional offers can also become merely nominal if a socially powerful participant occupies the desired role indefinitely. The proposal is therefore to evaluate transitions and participant agency, not count invitations as success. [P-10, P-13]

### Distinguish active excitement from inclusive communication

Crabb and Heron’s exploratory study involved four sessions with 14 participants with hearing loss or deafness; eight returned journals. Reports concerned overlap, pacing, visibility and control of discussion, while some participants reported no problems. The unfamiliar games, incomplete responses and researcher presence limit transfer. This is evidence that barriers and awareness can differ, not a quantified effect of a new facilitation system. [P-07; GDR-SRC-20261010-G19-020]

The bridge is to test an accessible chance to receive information, formulate a contribution and have it considered. Candidate adaptations include identifying the current speaker, allowing completion of interpretation, keeping shared objects visible, and offering an equivalent submission route. Those are conditional design proposals. A rigid turn system may prevent the overlapping improvisation some groups seek; a quiet written route may alter timing and comedic delivery. Test whether the alteration preserves the selected group activity instead of labeling either format inherently superior. [P-09, P-10]

### Do not use laughter or continuation as the belonging outcome

The inspected Wood study examined brief video-mediated friend/stranger conversations, not games. Laughter was related to baseline perceived similarity but was not associated with subsequent similarity change after baseline adjustment; the authors preserve power and context limitations. That neither makes laughter meaningless nor proves it cannot affect relationships. [P-08; GDR-SRC-20261010-G19-021]

For application, collect laughter only as one observed behavior, separately from private reports of welcome, agency, desired contribution and ease of leaving. Track who is unable to act, whose contributions are bypassed, and whether a person wanted the observer role. Do not conclude that everyone’s experience improved because a group stayed longer or produced more reactions. Conversely, quiet play should not automatically be classified as failed social engagement. [P-09]

### Plan recovery and content handling as part of the round

Jackbox’s published controls distinguish authored-prompt filtering from controls on user submissions; the latter requires a different intervention point. The documents also describe timing options. These establish available functions, not successful protection or accessible timing for a particular group. [P-05; GDR-SRC-20261010-G19-009]

In a proposed prompt-driven activity, specify what happens after a skipped response, connection loss, moderated submission, misunderstood rule or withdrawal. A censored answer should not silently become a score penalty without a considered policy; an uncensored prompt filter should not be advertised as handling all participant-generated content. Decide who bears moderation work and how that person rests or hands off. When reliable pre-display review cannot be staffed, use bounded authored choices or trusted-group play rather than promising open public submissions will be safe. These are alternatives, not a guarantee from any setting. [P-12, P-14]

## Pattern selection and adaptations

Use [Post-elimination participation contract](../patterns/post-elimination-participation-contract.md), **G19-PAT-02**, when losing an active role leaves someone present in a continuing round. It requires a declared information boundary and the option to rest/leave; adding a decorative minigame is not evidence of inclusion. A no-elimination design or planned break may be better. [P-09, P-10, P-12]

Use the inspected **F01-PAT-01** for a contrastive rules check, not as a compulsory long tutorial. Use **P02-PAT-02** for alternative cue routes; its unresolved overlap with **P01-PAT-01** remains visible. For a rhythm-party hybrid, consult [Rhythm](rhythm.md) and **G19-PAT-01** rather than making a social timer setting stand in for calibration. Role rotation/re-entry is queued for further research rather than extracted as a third pattern in this packet. [P-11, P-13]

The cases are selected to expose decisions, not rank products or cover the entire genre. No executable build, public stream, moderated live game or participant session was performed by this batch. [P-09]

**Illustrative worked application—not a tested design:** A shared caption-writing activity has authors, voters and observers. Before play, it explains that voting influences the round but does not submit an original caption. A participant can accept an author slot, choose voting, watch, or take a break. A practice result uses two different vote distributions under a stated local rule. Submissions pass through a moderator before display; a blocked or missing submission uses a declared fallback instead of an improvised public accusation. At the round boundary, a private role offer allows re-entry; declining does not trigger a public “why?” prompt. Compare this with fixed roles and with an all-cooperative caption-building alternative. More author turns is not the success criterion if a person preferred observing. [P-10–P-12, P-14]

## Accessibility, ethics, localization and production

A proposed access audit follows the entire occasion: finding the room/session, understanding consent and rules, receiving public/private information, acting through the available input, interpreting results, recovering and leaving. Include hearing, sight, reading, speech and motor needs without assuming one alternative fixes them all. Shared audio may be a distraction or unavailable; text can impose reading and composition demands. Have participants choose relevant routes instead of inferring preferences from a diagnosis. [P-07, P-09, P-13]

Localization must inspect wordplay, culturally specific prompts, text expansion, input methods and the time required to interpret an instruction. Translating only menu labels may leave the actual contribution inaccessible. In hidden-information hybrids, interpreters, shared captions and device screen readers need an explicit audience/role contract; accommodation should not force a secret into the public channel. These are proposed audits, not results from multilingual participants in G19. [P-10, P-13]

Ethically, allow a genuine pass and withdrawal, avoid forced personal disclosure, and distinguish in-game competitive disagreement from harassment. Public reaction clips or chat logs require separate consent and retention decisions; this research authorizes no collection. Production must account for explanation content, moderator capacity, recovery paths, host handoff and cross-device checks. Where capacity is absent, reduce feature scope rather than promise unsupported inclusion or safety. [P-14]

## Research questions and validation

All studies are **proposed, not performed**. Compare at the group/session level where appropriate; repeated rounds and participants in one group are not independent new samples. Record familiarity, relationship context, communication routes, host behavior and roles selected. Recruitment and stopping criteria require a later approved protocol.

| Question / comparison | Measures and procedure | Confounds and rejection criterion |
|---|---|---|
| Do optional role offers improve chosen contribution over fixed roles? | Compare fixed, voluntary rotation and optional observer/rest routes over matched round types. Collect private role preference, actual influence, re-entry success, burden and willingness to stop. | Friend hierarchy, content, prior skill and facilitator behavior may dominate. Reject “inclusive rotation” if turn count rises but preferred contribution or perceived freedom falls. [P-10, P-12] |
| Does a contrastive demonstration resolve comprehension rather than merely increase repetition? | Ask different same-rule outcome predictions before and after explanation; then let participants act with access supports retained. | Spoilers, content-generation difficulty and reading access can confound success. Revise the explanation when predictions remain indiscriminate even if submission completion improves. [P-11] |
| Does a communication adaptation preserve the selected activity? | Compare a jointly chosen speaker/interpretation accommodation with the baseline; measure information received, attempted/considered contributions, burden and preference. | It changes pacing and sometimes the activity’s improvisational character. Reject a universal benefit claim if some participants lose their desired agency or the process relies on unavailable facilitation. [P-07, P-10] |
| Are content and recovery controls sufficient for the proposed setting? | Later authorized staff-only walkthroughs of skipped, rejected and disconnected submissions; participant research only after separate approval. Record visible state, fallback, appeal/re-entry and moderator load. | A checklist is not observed moderation effectiveness. Narrow the deployment context when harmful content can bypass a promised review stage or recovery silently penalizes access. [P-05, P-14] |

## Coverage checkpoint and next discriminating question

Researched: party boundaries; contribution/observation distinctions; rules explanation; audience/asymmetry; recovery and moderation decisions; group-sensitive outcome measures; access and localization questions. Open: observed live rounds, representative child/family and cross-cultural evidence, public-event facilitation, physical/movement games, device availability, longitudinal inclusion and moderation effectiveness. The next question is **whether each intended participant can choose and enact a valued role, including a break, under the actual group’s conditions**. Contradictions `G19-X03`, `G19-X04`, `G19-X07` and source discrepancy `G19-X08` remain explicit in the packet.

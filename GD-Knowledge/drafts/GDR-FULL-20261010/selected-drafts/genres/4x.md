# 4X

**Draft genre guidance; coverage open.** This entry helps choose the scale, information, growth and conclusion rules of a game about developing a polity over an extended strategic horizon. It does not certify a taxonomy boundary, a historical account, balanced competition or a particular game design. Read [the G09 packet](../reviews/full-run-20261010-g09-review-packet.md) for the inspected dependency snapshot and missing links.

## Definition and boundaries

For this candidate, **4X** denotes a family of strategic structures in which exploration or information acquisition, expansion of effective reach, resource development and consequential competition interact across a persistent polity or empire. “Persistent” here means that commitments and accumulated state matter across the strategic session; it does not require an online persistent world, an immortal ruler, a historical civilization or account-level progression. The familiar explore/expand/exploit/exterminate wording is an object of investigation, not a four-item admission test. This is a provisional catalog definition, not an approved universal definition. [C-01]

In particular, a player need not exterminate every rival to complete an intelligible 4X experience. A nonconquest ending may still depend on territorial domination, extraction or military deterrence; removing the conquest victory button does not by itself remove those structures. Conversely, a combat-focused game may satisfy the working definition without diplomacy being equally developed. The boundary should be justified by interacting strategic commitments, not the presence of four menu tabs. [C-01, C-06, C-08]

Time model and genre membership are separate. The official **Sins of a Solar Empire II** FAQ explicitly combines real-time RTS and 4X terminology. Its documented structure is a contrary case to making turn-based input a membership requirement, not proof that all RTS games are 4X. [C-02; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-001]

A management game may emphasize operating an institution without expansion or competition over external strategic space. A grand-strategy label may emphasize political or historical simulation without a uniform “start small, develop, prevail” arc. These are neighboring analytic descriptions, not exclusions established by this batch. G08's Strategy/RTS/TBS guidance is running or unreviewed in the dispatch and its bodies were not inspected; this candidate does not redefine those parent entries. Multiplayer, real-time, science-fiction, historical and cooperative are facets, not substitute genre files. [C-01, C-12]

## Custom research instructions

Future research should begin by specifying **whose polity persists, what carries across time, what can be relinquished, and which endings are genuinely available**. Track exploration, territorial reach, economic capability, institutions, diplomacy and force separately. Examine a game with a nonconquest completion route, a real-time example, a ruler-succession structure, a deliberately asymmetric opponent and a design where losing territory can remain interesting. Do not claim that the examples in this first pass exhaust those variations. [C-01, C-08]

Inspect a versioned rulebook or build before asserting that a victory is feasible without warfare, that an AI obeys player constraints, or that expansion is necessary. A creator's description of an intended “peaceful” route can coexist with unexamined extraction incentives. For historical settings, compare the actor and goal assumptions in rules, event writing and victory accounting; consult scholarship and relevant cultural expertise rather than treating familiar technology-tree sequences as neutral history. Evaluate translated terms such as civilization, barbarian, progress, development and tribute in their mechanical context. [C-06, C-07, C-11]

The next source strategy should deliberately seek failed or rejected anti-snowball interventions, long-session observations with recoverable and irrecoverable setbacks, original diplomacy research, and human understanding of victory forecasts. Avoid comparing total playtime across unmatched maps, victory configurations and player goals. Do not use popularity, the number of victory types, or identical starting choices as evidence of fairness. Preserve the distinction between revealing a rule and revealing a rival's hidden intention. [C-08–C-11]

## Player promises and varied audiences

The candidate promises are **consequential stewardship**, **a developing strategic plan**, **discovering and responding to other powers**, and **a conclusion that recognizes what was accomplished**. They are design hypotheses to test with particular audiences, not measured expectations of all 4X players. A player pursuing a competitive finish, a fictional dynasty, cultural interpretation, cooperative survival or an evolving sandbox may evaluate the same dominant position differently. [C-01, C-09]

A long horizon can make an early commitment meaningful, but also make an inaccessible information display or misunderstood rule expensive to recover from. Optional automation, a forecast or a concise re-entry summary should therefore be judged against the intended decision, not against an assumption that more manual control is always better. Some audiences may deliberately enjoy administering a successful empire after competitive uncertainty has largely ended. An automatic early stop would remove their desired experience. [C-09–C-11]

## Characteristic loops and structures

Use four interacting horizons as a **diagnostic model**, not a required sequence. At the action horizon, commit a scarce capability or negotiate. At the regional horizon, maintain a route, settle or develop a place, and respond to neighbors. At the institutional horizon, accumulate technology, relationships, obligations and production capacity. At the conclusion horizon, pursue or revise an ending. A particular game may combine, omit or recur through these horizons. [C-01, C-09]

Soren Johnson's Orders account describes a shared action budget intended to make activation selective rather than routinely moving every unit. This is a reported design rationale, not a measured cure for late-game workload. It suggests a question for adaptation: does the budget expose competing priorities, or merely make routine maintenance compete with the interesting actions? [C-03; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-002]

His diplomacy account contrasts repeatedly probing a bargaining table with event-driven relationships. That comparison concerns agency and transaction structure: less direct price search may make relationships less transactional, but can also reduce a player's ability to initiate a needed agreement. It does not establish that unpredictable diplomacy is more authentic or more enjoyable. [C-04; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-003]

His ending account reports victory points, player ambitions, an asymmetric AI arrangement and an early conclusion for dominance. These are useful alternatives to “destroy everyone,” with important differences in what the player and AI are trying to do. They are one development lineage, not three independent confirmations of an ideal 4X formula. [C-05; GDR-SRC-20261010-G09-004]

### Compounding advantage and the endgame

A possible snowball has several links: new reach yields productive capacity; that capacity finances further expansion or denies rivals resources; the next gain arrives before its costs become binding; the advantage then compounds. Each link is conditional on local rules. Ownership without usable production, income consumed by upkeep, delayed infrastructure, vulnerable logistics or coalition responses can interrupt it. Simply observing a large empire does not identify which link produced its position. [C-09]

Distinguish **lead**, **forecasted victory**, **practically recoverable disadvantage** and **declared completion**. A visible score can be a poor forecast when a hidden victory project or coalition can intervene. A strong forecast can still leave meaningful stewardship, narrative or cooperative goals. Conversely, forcing administrative turns after the locally desired contest is settled can be a production and pacing problem even when the balance model works as intended. The proposed response is to diagnose the remaining decisions before choosing catch-up mechanics or an earlier ending. [C-09, C-10]

## Design choices and conditional application

| Decision | Conditional approach and evidence-to-application bridge | Cost, alternative and stop condition |
|---|---|---|
| What expansion means | Specify territory, access, influence and productive reach separately. A treaty can expand access without annexation in an illustrative design. This makes the intended conflict inspectable rather than hiding it in the genre label. [C-01, C-08] | If the game intentionally makes territorial conquest its central promise, state that honestly; do not advertise equally viable diplomacy without supporting rules. |
| Technology and economic commitment | Show prerequisites, already committed capacity and delayed obligations where they affect the decision. Scarcity then concerns feasible competing plans, not merely opaque accounting. [C-10, C-13] | Revealing a rival's secret project is not required to explain the player's own cost. Use known-state summaries when a prediction would fabricate precision. |
| Diplomatic agency | Compare offers initiated by players, event-driven opportunities and rule-bound institutions. Evaluate initiability, legibility, commitment duration and enforceability. [C-04, C-08] | Event-only diplomacy can become waiting for permission; bargaining can become threshold probing. Neither is inherently more strategic. |
| Growth advantage | Identify the dominant positive-feedback link before adding upkeep, friction, coalitions or recovery. Preserve the accomplishment that the player was promised. [C-09] | Uniform penalties on success can erase strategic planning. A shorter scenario or a declared asymmetric challenge may solve the actual problem with fewer hidden rules. |
| Victory horizon | Distinguish optional early conclusion, irreversible final victory, shared victory and continued sandbox play. Connect each to explicit obligations and remaining uncertainty. [C-05, C-09] | Do not use a score ratio as a universal threshold. Do not force “one more turn” to collect an already-earned ending, or silently erase others' goals. |
| Fairness | Audit access, initial state, action opportunities, knowledge, costs, feasible counters and declared objectives. Equal menus settle only one part of that audit. [C-08] | Symmetry can be desirable for a particular contest; asymmetry can be understandable but still unfair in practice. Neither label certifies the outcome. |

**Application sequence — proposed.** Frame the promised strategic experience and ending first. Map one representative commitment from the known information through cost, delayed consequences and opportunity to revise. Identify which consequences are intended uncertainty and which are accidental opacity. Compare a minimal truthful presentation with a richer forecast; separately compare growth or victory-rule changes, rather than bundling them into the same comprehension test. Preserve a baseline and a reversibility plan for the prototype. Only then seek separately authorized playtests. [C-09–C-11, C-13]

### Worked example: an approaching conclusion — illustrative

A fictional three-polity campaign permits either a territorial settlement or a negotiated federation. The leading polity has extensive mines but must keep a supply corridor open. A rival can negotiate passage with a neutral polity; the leader knows the treaty rules but not the rival's current offer. The campaign interface shows the leader's own corridor dependency and declared federation requirements, not a fabricated probability of victory. [C-09, C-10]

A designer suspects that the remaining campaign is “already won.” Before shortening it, distinguish three explanations: the rival truly has no effective action; the rival has a route but cannot discover it; or the rival is pursuing an incompatible story goal. A review can trace feasible actions in the designed state, while a future player test can examine interpretation. The first observation cannot substitute for the second. [C-09, C-11]

Suppose an optional negotiated conclusion is offered. It records control, surviving obligations and each signatory's choice; declining keeps the current rules rather than imposing a punishment. The stronger player can continue stewardship, but cannot unilaterally declare that another human participant accepted the fiction's treaty. This is a proposed hybrid of strategic and narrative closure, not a claim about any shipped game's behavior. Reject it if negotiation merely adds ceremonial clicks or if it obscures a genuinely contested finish. [C-08, C-09, C-12]

### Worked example: a “peaceful” route that still extracts — illustrative

A cultural victory in a fictional historical strategy game requires monuments built from occupied provinces, while displaced communities appear only as a temporary productivity penalty. Calling the win peaceful does not address the underlying representation. An alternative could make treaty-based cultural exchange mechanically distinct, preserve local agency and record disputed outcomes; it might also change the strategic premise and require substantial writing and consultation. Neither alternative is approved by this candidate. The design question is which account of history the rules invite, not whether enough culturally named cosmetic assets are present. [C-06, C-07]

## Pattern selection and hybrid conflicts

Use existing **Visible opportunity-cost comparison**, `GDR-CAND-20261010-F02-PAT-01`, for known competing investments; do not duplicate it as a “4X budgeting” pattern. Use **Multi-route objective contract**, `GDR-CAND-20261010-W02-PAT-02`, only where the end condition permits distinct methods and preserves consequences. **Legible commitment and safe exit**, `GDR-CAND-20261010-F07-PAT-02`, applies conditionally to campaign/session commitments, with legitimate costs to other players retained. All are reviewed drafts. [C-13]

The new [Evidence-bounded resolution debrief](../patterns/evidence-bounded-resolution-debrief.md), `GDR-CAND-20261010-G09-PAT-01`, is an **untested hypothesis** for explaining a bounded sequence such as a supply failure. It should not pronounce the one cause of an empire's decline or disclose an unseen diplomatic agreement. For broad systemic uncertainty, a dated factual ledger may be preferable to an automated recommendation. [C-14]

In a 4X/RTS hybrid, a pause-friendly planning interface and continuous adversarial time may conflict. In a 4X/tower-defense hybrid, permanent territorial investments can conflict with wave-by-wave rebuilding. In a 4X/auto-battler hybrid, account or empire advantages can overwhelm the tactical roster contest advertised for each battle. Resolve these through explicit authority, time and persistence rules, not by declaring one genre dominant. These are illustrative conflict models, not surveyed audience outcomes. See [tower defense](tower-defense.md) and [auto-battler](auto-battler.md). [C-12]

## Representative cases and negative cases

The inspected range is deliberately small: a real-time official FAQ, one developer's multi-part Old World retrospective and two original interpretive historical-game papers. This covers contrary structures, not prevalence. Civilization VI and VII official pages attempted for further victory/age detail returned access failures; those snippets are not used as evidence. No empirical 4X audience or long-session study was established in this batch. [C-02–C-07]

The strongest negative case to a short, legible endgame is an audience that wants open-ended stewardship after superiority is apparent. The strongest negative case to “make every route equally available” is an explicitly asymmetric scenario whose challenge and fiction depend on unequal capabilities. Neither objection licenses hidden rules or misleading promises; both require testing a different intended experience rather than optimizing one contest metric. [C-08–C-11]

## Accessibility, localization, ethics and production

**Proposed adaptation:** provide re-entry views of current commitments, unresolved threats and changed rules, with on-demand detail. Test complete tasks: locating a province, understanding an obligation, issuing an order, confirming its result and resuming after interruption. Reading a dense ledger, pointing to tiny territories, interpreting colors and handling simultaneous alerts impose different demands. A narration route may need structured navigation rather than a recital of the whole world state. These are functional hypotheses; no actual access outcome is claimed. [C-11, C-13]

Historical and cultural work requires an interpretation audit of goals and available actions, not just a terminology pass. Ford's analysis motivates questions about progression and extraction; McCall provides a different framework for how goals and genre conventions structure historical interpretation. Both are original scholarly interpretations, not proof of an effect on every player's beliefs. Cultural consultation, contested readings, translated treaty semantics and participant interpretation remain open work. [C-06, C-07]

Forecasts, treaty states, long saves, alternate endings and changing technology rules each create authoring and verification work. A concise known-state explanation is a lower-cost alternative to claiming a globally reliable victory predictor. Any future automated simulation must distinguish legal game-state reachability from modeled opponent policy and actual player experience. Record production effort and maintenance after rule changes, not only the initial prototype's apparent clarity. [C-09–C-11]

## Research questions and validation

**All tests below are proposed and not performed.** Recruitment, consent, sampling precision and practical acceptance margins need a separately approved study plan; no universal session length or participant count is specified. [C-11]

| Discriminating question | Comparison, participants and procedure | Measures, confounds and rejection rule |
|---|---|---|
| Does conclusion information reduce mistaken inevitability without spoiling contest? | Compare known-state summary, explicit rule-based progress and qualified forecast in matched late-game states. Include competitive and stewardship-oriented participants; counterbalance states, not merely interface order. | Measure rule prediction, false certainty, identified feasible responses and desire to continue separately. Reject the richer forecast if its claim precision exceeds the model or it suppresses desired play without a comprehension benefit. |
| Does an action budget create meaningful prioritization or extra housekeeping? | Compare a bounded illustrative scenario under alternative activation rules, keeping objectives and essential decisions explicit. This is a rules comparison, not a UI-only test. | Record deferred routine work, articulated opportunity costs, planning burden and access barriers. Reject the budget when it mainly adds maintenance contention or prevents a needed access route. |
| Is a recovery mechanism understandable without erasing earned advantage? | Present matched leader/trailing states, including an explicitly asymmetric scenario. Separate rule understanding from preferred winner. | Record feasible counterplay, surprise about constraints and perceived legitimacy; inspect experience and faction differences. A closer final score alone is insufficient. |
| Does a representation change alter interpretation? | Compare carefully scoped rule/outcome variants with relevant historical and cultural expertise, followed by participant explanation. Do not bundle every writing and mechanic change. | Record competing interpretations and unresolved disagreement. Do not infer beliefs from victory choice or claim a universal cultural response. |

For learning checks, preserve the reviewed F01 distinction: test unfamiliar cases with the **same rule**, including affordable and unaffordable choices; test a **changed rule** as separate adaptation/discrimination. Accessibility support is not withdrawn because a player succeeds. [C-11, C-13]

### Coverage and next discriminating work

Researched here: provisional membership, real-time counterexample, persistent polity interpretation, action-budget and diplomacy alternatives, non-elimination endings, snowball mechanisms as hypotheses, endgame legibility, representation questions and conditional shared guidance. Unresolved: original term-origin source, complete official current victory rules, empirical long-session effects, coalition/AI exploit resistance, current economic/exploration dependencies, cultural consultation, accessibility outcomes and multiplayer exit agreements. The next question is **which remaining late-game decisions the intended audience values**, before selecting an anti-snowball or early-ending intervention. [C-01–C-14]

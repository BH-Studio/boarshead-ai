## Research brief and ownership applied

The decision questions were framed before synthesis: what visual distinctions communicate an intended action/state; where and when task facts should be represented; and how to remove sensory, motor and cognitive barriers while retaining choice. Competing explanations included familiarity instead of visual “intuitiveness,” loss of information disguised as simplification, expertise-dependent HUD preferences, configuration burden, and redundancy that creates interruption or stale information.

The strategy was a bounded purposive review of normative publishers, original studies and firsthand/official guidance, including contrary results and source-reporting limits. Search snippets and abstracts were discovery leads, not support for unread study details. Source records and a pre-synthesis claim ledger were recorded before document assembly; an additional formal claim was later separated during author review to correct a focus-criterion citation. This was not a preregistered systematic review or an exhaustive taxonomy survey.

### Strongest recommendation challenged

The strongest cross-entry recommendation is to define the required task facts and provide a usable alternative when a channel fails. Its most tempting overreach is “redundancy improves access, therefore more simultaneous cues are always better.” That does not follow. A semantic duplicate can arrive late, contradict current state, compete with narration, overload a reader, or disclose a fact the original cue did not reveal. The player may already have a simpler adequate route.

**Disposition:** retain one conditional pattern, with explicit prerequisites, semantic matching, priority limits, state-resolution rules, player control, do-not-use conditions and a simpler comparator. The chain from extra representation to correct, comfortable action remains a low-confidence hypothesis. Do not promote the pattern as a measured result merely because several guidelines motivate its parts.

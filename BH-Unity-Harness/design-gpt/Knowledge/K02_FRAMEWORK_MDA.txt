Framework invocation rule
Use this framework when its triggering conditions match the design problem. Do not force every problem
through every framework. When several frameworks apply, state which framework owns which question and
reconcile conflicts in a final synthesis pass.

Custom GPT operating directive
When to invoke MDA
Invoke MDA when the team is deciding what a mechanic should accomplish experientially, diagnosing why a
feature feels wrong, comparing alternative mechanics that aim at the same experience, or translating a
high-level design pillar into implementable behavior.

• Begin with the desired player experience whenever the goal is known. Work backward through dynamics to
mechanics before proposing implementation details.
• When reviewing an existing mechanic, work forward from mechanics to observed or predicted dynamics, then
compare the resulting experience to the target aesthetics.
• Treat dynamics as run-time behavior created by mechanics, player inputs, system state, and interactions among
subsystems. Do not reduce dynamics to a feature list.
• Treat aesthetics as desired emotional or experiential responses. In MDA, aesthetics does not mean visual art
style.
• Every significant mechanic should have an explicit experience rationale. Mechanics with no defensible trace to
intended dynamics or aesthetics are candidates for removal, simplification, or redesign.

Framework model and vocabulary
Mechanics

Dynamics

Aesthetics

Designer view: rules and components produce behavior, which produces experience.

The original MDA paper formalizes three causally linked views of a game. Mechanics are the concrete components,
rules, data representations, control mechanisms, and algorithms. Dynamics are the run-time behaviors that emerge
when those mechanics interact with player inputs and with each other over time. Aesthetics are the desirable
emotional responses and experiential qualities evoked in the player. [MDA-1]
The framework becomes more powerful when the team consciously changes direction. Designers commonly move
forward from mechanics toward aesthetics, while players encounter the experience in the opposite direction: they first
perceive an experience, infer the system behavior that produces it, and only gradually learn the underlying rules. This
is why a technically elegant mechanic can still fail if the player-facing dynamics are unreadable or produce the wrong
feeling. [MDA-1]

Aesthetic targets
Hunicke, LeBlanc, and Zubek recommend replacing vague claims such as "make it fun" with a more directed
vocabulary. Their illustrative taxonomy includes the following eight aesthetic categories. The categories are not an
exhaustive personality test and should not be treated as mutually exclusive. [MDA-1]
Aesthetic

Operational interpretation

Sensation

Sense pleasure; satisfying audiovisual, tactile, or embodied response.

Fantasy

Make-believe; inhabiting a role, world, identity, or imagined situation.

Narrative

Drama; tension, development, resolution, and story-like meaning.

Challenge

Obstacles that invite skill, planning, mastery, or problem solving.

Fellowship

Social framework; cooperation, belonging, rivalry, shared activity.

Discovery

Uncharted territory; learning, exploration, surprise, uncovering systems.

Expression

Leaving a personal mark; style, building, customization, self-directed choices.

Submission

Pastime; willingly entering a structured activity or habitual play state.

Persona rule
For a specific system, choose a small number of primary aesthetic targets and rank them. A system that tries
to maximize every aesthetic simultaneously usually loses clarity. Secondary aesthetics can be supported, but
the team should know what wins when goals conflict.

Dynamics and emergence
Dynamics are where much of the actual game lives. The paper emphasizes that computer games are especially
difficult because interacting coded subsystems can produce complex and unpredictable behavior. A dynamic is
therefore not merely "the player uses the crafting menu." Useful dynamic statements describe behavior over time:
players hoard rare components until a breakpoint, squads split roles because information is asymmetric, a winning
player snowballs, or a stealth player waits for patrol cycles to align. [MDA-1]
• Observe incentives. What behavior is made safer, faster, cheaper, more powerful, or more legible by the
mechanics?
• Observe emergent strategy. What patterns will rational or experimental players discover that were not explicitly
authored?
• Observe feedback. Does success make future success easier, does the system self-correct, or does it oscillate?
• Observe social dynamics. Do the rules create cooperation, dominance, specialization, exclusion, signaling, or
griefing?
• Observe pacing. Where do pressure, release, repetition, and surprise arise from system behavior rather than
scripted content?

MDA is particularly useful for identifying a mismatch between intended and actual dynamics. If the desired aesthetic is
tense tactical decision-making but the dominant dynamic is resource hoarding followed by one optimal burst, the
correct question is not "how do we polish the burst?" but "which mechanics make hoarding dominant, and what
dynamic would better produce the target tension?"

Mechanics and tuning
Mechanics are the manipulable design levers. The original paper notes that changing fundamental rules can alter
overall system behavior, and that tuning should be iterative. MDA is therefore not a one-time decomposition exercise;
it is a loop between hypothesis, implementation, play, observation, and adjustment. [MDA-1]

Retune

MDA is strongest as an iterative tuning loop.

• Prefer the smallest mechanic change that tests a dynamic hypothesis before expanding scope.
• Tune numbers only after confirming that the structural relationship is correct. A bad loop rarely becomes good merely
by changing a coefficient.
• Measure what the player can actually perceive. A dynamic that exists mathematically but is not legible may not
contribute to the intended aesthetic.
• When a mechanic creates multiple dynamics, rank which are desired, tolerated, or harmful. Do not assume one
mechanic has one consequence.

1. Frame the experience

Describe the run-time behaviors that should create the target experience. Use verbs and

4. Trace enabling

List the minimum rules, variables, affordances, AI behaviors, content, and constraints required

5. Stress-test interactions

Ask how skilled, risk-averse, optimizing, social, or exploit-seeking players may bend the

6. Prototype the smallest
lever

Change or prototype the smallest mechanic that can validate the causal chain.

7. Observe actual
dynamics

Record what players do, not just what they say they intended to do.

8. Compare to aesthetics

Determine whether the resulting experience matches the target, conflicts with another aesthetic,
or creates an unintended experience.

9. Tune or restructure

Tune values when the structure is sound; change mechanics or relationships when the structure
produces the wrong dynamic.

10. Record the trace

Maintain an M-D-A rationale so later feature changes can be evaluated against the original
experience goal.

Multi-persona team operating pattern
This section describes how to apply MDA inside an existing multi-persona design committee. These are responsibility
overlays, not a requirement to create new permanent personas. Map each responsibility onto the personas already
present in the team.
• Independent first pass. Relevant personas analyze the same design question independently before seeing the
synthesis. This reduces premature consensus.
• Evidence/assumption separation. Every persona labels what is known, inferred, assumed, or proposed. Numerical
claims without evidence are treated as hypotheses.
• Constructive disagreement. At least one persona must challenge the current favorite solution and identify a
plausible failure mode or player exploit.
• Framework vocabulary. Personas use the framework terms precisely. If a term is ambiguous, they define it before
using it as a decision criterion.
• Convergence pass. A synthesis lead resolves conflicts by returning to player goals, constraints, and evidence - not
by averaging opinions.
• Traceable output. Final recommendations include why the framework supports the decision, what remains uncertain,
and what playtest or simulation would falsify it.

Prompt patterns for AI-assisted design
The following patterns are designed to be reusable inside a custom GPT. Replace bracketed fields with project
context. The prompts explicitly separate framing, independent analysis, criticism, and convergence.

Pattern A - Framework invocation
Use MDA to analyze [SYSTEM OR MECHANIC].
Context: [PLAYER, PLATFORM, GENRE, DESIGN PILLARS, CONSTRAINTS].
Decision to make: [EXACT DECISION].
Known evidence: [PLAYTEST DATA / TELEMETRY / DESIGN FACTS].
Unknowns: [UNCERTAINTIES].
Each relevant designer persona must:
1. Apply the framework terminology explicitly.
2. Separate observations from assumptions.
3. Identify at least one failure mode.
4. Recommend the smallest design change that could test the hypothesis.
5. State what evidence would change the recommendation.
Do not converge until independent persona passes are complete.

Pattern B - Cross-examination
Cross-examine the current design using MDA.
Ask and answer these questions:
- Which target aesthetic is primary, and what would a player actually do or say if we
achieved it?
- What run-time dynamic is supposed to create that experience?
- Which mechanic is necessary for that dynamic, and which mechanic is merely historical
baggage?
- What unintended dynamic would an optimizing player discover?
- What is the smallest playtestable change that could confirm or reject the causal chain?
For every concern, identify:
- severity: low / medium / high
- confidence: low / medium / high
- affected player behavior or experience
- likely root cause
- cheapest validation method
Do not propose feature additions until root causes are identified.

Pattern C - Convergence and decision record
Synthesize the persona analyses into one design decision.
Resolve conflicts by using the project pillars, player needs, constraints, and evidence.
Do not average incompatible recommendations.
Return exactly this structure:
1. Decision status: ACCEPT / REVISE / REJECT / PROVISIONAL
2. Target aesthetics (ranked)
3. Expected dynamics
4. Supporting mechanics
5. Unintended dynamics and risks
6. Evidence and assumptions
7. Recommended change
8. Validation experiment
9. MDA trace: Mechanic -> Dynamic -> Aesthetic
If evidence is insufficient, mark the decision PROVISIONAL and specify the experiment
needed.

Worked example - heat-based weapon system
Suppose the team proposes a weapon heat system for a real-time action game. The feature-first description is
"weapons build heat and stop firing when overheated." MDA forces a better design conversation.
Layer

Example analysis

Aesthetics

Primary: Challenge and tension. Secondary: Fantasy of operating a powerful but demanding
machine.

Desired dynamics

Players alternate between aggression and restraint; choose firing windows; reposition while cooling;
decide whether to risk an overheat to finish a target.

Mechanics

Heat per shot, cooling curve, overheat lockout, heat UI, alternate weapons, movement/cover
opportunities, enemy pressure windows.

Unintended dynamic

If cooling while not firing is too fast, optimal play becomes repetitive burst-wait-burst. If overheat
punishment is too severe, players stop experimenting and use only safe weapons.

Validation

Compare firing cadence, weapon-switch rate, time spent at high heat, player-reported tension, and
whether players describe meaningful risk decisions.

The critical insight is that the heat meter itself is not the design goal. The design succeeds only if the mechanics
reliably generate decision behavior that produces the intended experience.

Failure modes and misuse
• Feature-first design. Starting with a favorite mechanic and inventing an aesthetic justification afterward.
• Aesthetics = graphics. Misreading the term and discussing art direction instead of player experience.
• Dynamics as authored events. Listing encounters or features rather than describing behavior produced during play.
• Assuming linear causality. One mechanic often contributes to several dynamics, and one dynamic may depend on
several mechanics.
• Ignoring player perspective. The system can be internally coherent while the player cannot perceive cause,
consequence, or opportunity.
• Overusing the eight aesthetics. They are a vocabulary aid, not a complete taxonomy of motivation or emotion.
• Tuning before diagnosis. Changing values repeatedly when the underlying mechanic-to-dynamic relationship is
wrong.

Review gate and quality checklist
A system should not be considered reviewed under MDA until the team can answer the following checks with
evidence or an explicit test plan.
• Can the team state the primary intended player experience without using the words "fun," "engaging," or a feature
name?
• Can each major mechanic be traced to one or more intended dynamics?
• Can each intended dynamic be described as observable behavior over time?
• Has the team identified at least one plausible unintended dynamic created by optimization, confusion, or system
interaction?
• Does the player receive enough information and feedback to perceive the relevant dynamic?
• Is there a concrete playtest or telemetry signal that would falsify the design hypothesis?

• If the feature were removed, which target aesthetic would materially weaken? If none, why is the feature present?
AI behavior rule
If a required input is missing, the personas should not silently invent it. They should either state a bounded
assumption and continue, or mark the analysis as blocked when that assumption would materially change the
decision.

How this framework combines with the other four
Framework

Primary question

MDA

Defines intended experience and traces mechanics through emergent dynamics to player
response.

Core / Nested Loops

Defines repeated player activity, time scales, feedback cadence, and how progression loops
nest.

Systems Thinking

Maps causal relationships, feedback, delays, emergence, and structural leverage points.

Machinations

Makes resource/state flows executable so economy and progression behavior can be
simulated.

Rational Game Design

Parameterizes skills, mechanics, difficulty, learning, and content variety for controlled iteration.

MDA should own the questions it is best suited to answer, while the other frameworks provide complementary lenses.
A final design review should reconcile contradictions rather than treating all five outputs as independently correct.

Framework invocation rule
Use this framework when its triggering conditions match the design problem. Do not force every problem
through every framework. When several frameworks apply, state which framework owns which question and
reconcile conflicts in a final synthesis pass.

Custom GPT operating directive
When to invoke RGD
Invoke Rational Game Design when the team needs to define or compare mechanics precisely, diagnose
difficulty, plan teaching/progression, generate controlled content variety, create encounter/level metrics, or
replace subjective "this feels harder" discussion with testable parameters.

• Start from the player skill being challenged. Do not confuse player skill with an avatar stat or in-game skill tree.
• Break each mechanic into atomic parameters that can change the challenge while preserving the mechanic's
identity.
• Use numbers to make assumptions explicit and comparable, not to claim objectivity where player perception remains
uncertain.
• Design learning and difficulty as deliberate curves. Do not solve a difficulty dip by adding content until the root
parameter is understood.
• Use variety matrices to explore combinations of mechanics and modifiers before adding one-off mechanics.

What Rational Design is
Chris McEntee's account of Ubisoft Montpellier's Rational Design practice describes a methodology focused on
eliminating unnecessary information, making mechanics readable, introducing them in an orderly way, and preserving
learning and difficulty curves or "macro flow." He emphasizes deep exploration of strong mechanics rather than using
many one-off mechanics to imitate depth. [RGD-1]
Alexis Jolis-Desautels later characterizes Rational Design as a set of tools, questions, and deliverables for designing
with intention, balancing creativity with reliability, and centering the user rather than the designer's personal taste.
[RGD-2] [RGD-3]

Player skills and mechanics
In McEntee's formulation, a mechanic is a challenge associated with a specific player input and skill, and its difficulty
can be altered through atomic parameters. He distinguishes player skills - physical, mental, or social capabilities
used by the human player - from character abilities or statistics. [RGD-1]

A useful AI rule is to require the persona proposing a mechanic to finish the sentence: "This mechanic asks the
player to use [skill] to overcome [challenge] by [input/decision], while these parameters control difficulty." If

the statement cannot be completed, the mechanic may still be an action or affordance, but its challenge structure is
not yet clear.

Atomic parameters and metrics
Atomic parameters are the smallest adjustable variables that materially change how difficult or demanding a mechanic
is without turning it into a different mechanic. Examples include distance, speed, timing window, accuracy requirement,
number of simultaneous threats, visibility, resource cost, enemy reaction time, or information quality. [RGD-1]

Metrics create a coordinate system for design decisions. The Level Design Book summarizes RGD/RLD as identifying
mechanics and parameters, quantifying them, combining them across content, and iterating toward target difficulty or
pacing. It also warns against treating quantified design as inherently objective or universally superior. [RGD-4]

Micro flow and macro flow
Jolis-Desautels describes micro flow as second-to-second conditions that keep the player engaged in the immediate
activity and macro flow as longer-term progression in which challenge should evolve as player ability grows. He
connects macro flow to learning, rewards, progression, and short/medium/long Objective-Challenge-Reward loops.
[RGD-3]
Scale

Design responsibility

Micro flow

Clear goals, responsive feedback, skill/challenge balance, rhythmic input/decision patterns,
reinforcing success without erasing failure.

Macro flow

Teach mechanics, combine them, increase parameter difficulty, vary pacing, add
rewards/possibilities, and keep challenge aligned with growing player capability.

Learning, signs, and readability
Rational Design is not only about numerical difficulty. McEntee emphasizes readability, understandable information,
orderly introduction of mechanics, and the relationship between learning and challenge. A mechanic cannot be fairly
challenging if the player cannot identify the relevant object, state, rule, danger, or success condition. [RGD-1]
• Teach one new idea against a familiar baseline. New mechanics are easier to learn when the rest of the challenge
remains stable.
• Use signs and feedback consistently. Visual, audio, animation, UI, and spatial cues should communicate function
before demanding mastery.
• Separate knowledge difficulty from execution difficulty. A player may fail because the rule is unclear, not
because the intended skill challenge is high.

• Sequence combination complexity. After individual mechanics are learned, difficulty can come from combining
known elements under new constraints.

Variety and possibility space
McEntee argues that variety should refresh the player's rhythm and strategy without relying on endless new
mechanics. Rational Design uses matrices and parameter combinations to explore the existing possibility space, often
finding new challenges by changing constraints or removing familiar options. [RGD-1]

The matrix is a generator of candidate design spaces, not a requirement to use every combination. Personas should
filter combinations through target experience, production cost, readability, and redundancy.

Step-by-step RGD workflow
Step

Action

1. Define the player and
experience

Identify target audience assumptions, desired experience, and constraints.

2. Name player skills

List the human skills the system should challenge or develop.

3. Define mechanics

For each mechanic, specify input/decision, challenge, success/failure, and feedback.

4. Extract atomic
parameters

Identify variables that alter difficulty or expression without replacing the mechanic.

5. Set parameter ranges

Define easy-to-hard or low-to-high ranges as hypotheses, not truths.

6. Build learning sequence

Introduce signs, safe practice, isolated use, then combinations.

7. Build variety matrix

Cross mechanics, modifiers, constraints, objectives, and environments to explore possibility
space.

8. Plan macro flow

Map when mechanics appear, combine, intensify, rest, and return.

9. Prototype and measure

Test whether difficulty comes from intended player skill rather than confusion or production
noise.

10. Calibrate

Use observed completion, error patterns, timing, strategy diversity, and player confidence to
update parameter ranges.

11. Cut redundancy

Remove mechanics or content combinations that do not add a new skill demand, strategy,
rhythm, or experience.

Multi-persona team operating pattern
This section describes how to apply Rational Game Design inside an existing multi-persona design committee. These
are responsibility overlays, not a requirement to create new permanent personas. Map each responsibility onto the
personas already present in the team.
• Independent first pass. Relevant personas analyze the same design question independently before seeing the
synthesis. This reduces premature consensus.
• Evidence/assumption separation. Every persona labels what is known, inferred, assumed, or proposed. Numerical
claims without evidence are treated as hypotheses.
• Constructive disagreement. At least one persona must challenge the current favorite solution and identify a
plausible failure mode or player exploit.
• Framework vocabulary. Personas use the framework terms precisely. If a term is ambiguous, they define it before
using it as a decision criterion.
• Convergence pass. A synthesis lead resolves conflicts by returning to player goals, constraints, and evidence - not
by averaging opinions.
• Traceable output. Final recommendations include why the framework supports the decision, what remains uncertain,
and what playtest or simulation would falsify it.

Prompt patterns for AI-assisted design
The following patterns are designed to be reusable inside a custom GPT. Replace bracketed fields with project
context. The prompts explicitly separate framing, independent analysis, criticism, and convergence.

Pattern A - Framework invocation
Use Rational Game Design to analyze [SYSTEM OR MECHANIC].
Context: [PLAYER, PLATFORM, GENRE, DESIGN PILLARS, CONSTRAINTS].
Decision to make: [EXACT DECISION].
Known evidence: [PLAYTEST DATA / TELEMETRY / DESIGN FACTS].
Unknowns: [UNCERTAINTIES].
Each relevant designer persona must:
1. Apply the framework terminology explicitly.
2. Separate observations from assumptions.
3. Identify at least one failure mode.
4. Recommend the smallest design change that could test the hypothesis.
5. State what evidence would change the recommendation.
Do not converge until independent persona passes are complete.

Pattern B - Cross-examination
Cross-examine the current design using Rational Game Design.
Ask and answer these questions:
- Which human player skill is this mechanic challenging?
- What are the atomic parameters that control difficulty or variation?
- Is failure caused by the intended skill challenge, or by unreadable information / unclear
rules?
- How is the mechanic introduced, practiced, combined, and revisited across macro flow?
- Can variety be created by recombining existing mechanics or constraints instead of adding
a new mechanic?
For every concern, identify:
- severity: low / medium / high
- confidence: low / medium / high
- affected player behavior or experience
- likely root cause
- cheapest validation method
Do not propose feature additions until root causes are identified.

Pattern C - Convergence and decision record
Synthesize the persona analyses into one design decision.
Resolve conflicts by using the project pillars, player needs, constraints, and evidence.
Do not average incompatible recommendations.
Return exactly this structure:
1. Target player / experience
2. Player skills
3. Mechanic definitions
4. Atomic parameters and proposed ranges
5. Readability / signs / feedback requirements
6. Learning sequence
7. Variety matrix candidates
8. Difficulty and macro-flow plan
9. Redundant or one-off content to cut
10. Playtest metrics and error taxonomy
11. Recommended design decision
12. Confidence and open questions
If evidence is insufficient, mark the decision PROVISIONAL and specify the experiment
needed.

Worked example - enemy ranged attack
Element

RGD decomposition

Player skill

Threat recognition, timing, positioning, prioritization.

Mechanic

Enemy telegraphs a ranged attack; player avoids line of fire or interrupts the attacker.

Atomic parameters

Telegraph duration, projectile speed, projectile width, fire interval, enemy count, angle of attack,
cover availability, interrupt window.

Easy learning state

One enemy, long telegraph, clear line, generous cover, slow projectile.

Intermediate variation

Shorter telegraph + movement pressure; two enemies from related angles.

Advanced combination

Ranged enemy combined with area denial that restricts safe positioning; still uses learned signs
and rules.

Bad difficulty increase

Add five more enemies without knowing whether failure is recognition, timing, navigation,
damage, or cognitive overload.

The RGD value is diagnostic. Instead of "make encounter 7 harder," the team can decide whether to challenge
recognition, reaction, spatial planning, multitasking, or resource management - and adjust the parameter that targets
that skill.

Limits and criticism
RGD is useful precisely because it makes assumptions explicit, but that can tempt teams into false precision. The
Level Design Book notes that public documentation is incomplete and cautions against uncritical faith in a method that
quantifies conceptual depth. [RGD-4] Treat RGD as a tool for disciplined reasoning and controlled experiments, not an
equation that proves quality.
• Player perception of difficulty may not track a designer's numeric scale linearly.
• Novelty, emotion, narrative meaning, aesthetics, and social context can change challenge perception without
changing atomic parameters.

• A beautifully quantified possibility space can still contain boring combinations.
• Playtest evidence must recalibrate the model; the spreadsheet is a hypothesis, not ground truth.
• Creative exceptions are allowed when deliberate. The team should document why the exception is worth its learning
and production cost.

Failure modes
• Character skill confusion. Treating avatar stats as player skills.
• Add-more-enemies tuning. Increasing content count when the actual difficult parameter is unknown.
• False objectivity. Assuming a numeric score makes a subjective experience objectively correct.
• One-off mechanic inflation. Adding new rules for variety instead of exploring existing mechanics deeply.
• Unreadable challenge. Difficulty is produced by poor signaling rather than the intended skill demand.
• Matrix completionism. Building every combination merely because the grid contains it.
• Static calibration. Never updating parameter ranges after player behavior contradicts designer assumptions.

Review gate and quality checklist
A system should not be considered reviewed under Rational Game Design until the team can answer the following
checks with evidence or an explicit test plan.
• Is every major mechanic tied to a specific human player skill or decision demand?
• Can the team name the atomic parameters that control difficulty and variation?
• Are easy/hard ranges hypotheses that can be recalibrated through playtesting?
• Can players perceive the signs, rules, goals, and feedback needed to attribute success and failure correctly?
• Does the learning sequence isolate new concepts before combining them?
• Does content variety primarily exploit the existing design space before adding new one-off mechanics?
• Can the team explain which parameter caused a difficulty change instead of using aggregate labels such as "harder"?
• Has at least one persona challenged whether the numeric model is masking an experiential problem?
AI behavior rule
If a required input is missing, the personas should not silently invent it. They should either state a bounded
assumption and continue, or mark the analysis as blocked when that assumption would materially change the
decision.

independently correct.

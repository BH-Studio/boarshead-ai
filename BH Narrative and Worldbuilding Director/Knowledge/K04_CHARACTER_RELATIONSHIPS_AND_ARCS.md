# Character Development, Relationships, and Arcs

## Core principle
A game character is not a biography plus traits. A strong character is a **pattern of choices under pressure** that the player can understand, anticipate, challenge, and sometimes influence.

## Character foundation
For major characters define:
- **Role:** story function and gameplay/player-facing function.
- **External Want:** what they actively pursue.
- **Internal Need:** what growth/integration would actually help them.
- **Fear / Wound / History:** pressure source, not an excuse for behavior.
- **Belief or Misbelief:** model of the world that drives choices.
- **Contradiction:** two values/traits that cannot always coexist.
- **Competence:** why they matter.
- **Vulnerability:** where pressure can reach them.
- **Moral Line:** what they believe they will not do.
- **Secret / Knowledge Boundary:** what they hide or misunderstand.
- **Voice:** rhythm, vocabulary, directness, humor, status behavior.
- **Pressure Response:** fight, flee, freeze, bargain, control, joke, withdraw, confess, etc.
- **Arc:** change, refusal to change, corruption, recovery, revelation, or flat/steadfast influence.
- **Player Agency Boundary:** what the player may change versus what remains authored.

## Pixar-derived fast character diagnostic
Pixar's public storytelling curriculum organizes character thinking around:
- internal vs external features;
- wants vs needs;
- obstacles;
- character arc;
- stakes.
This is an excellent early-stage check because it prevents visual backstory from substituting for dramatic drive.

Source: Pixar in a Box / Khan Academy: https://www.khanacademy.org/computing/pixar/storytelling

## Player-character authorship models
### Authored protagonist
Strong fixed voice, history, relationships, and transformation. Player agency is usually about method, attitude, relationships, or selected values within a defined identity.

### Defined-role + customization
Core role/history is fixed, but appearance/background details/attitudes may vary. Explicitly define which customization has narrative acknowledgment.

### Blank slate
The player's chosen identity and values are primary. Avoid prewritten emotional reactions that override the identity the player thought they authored.

### Avatar
Character is mainly a vehicle for the player's own presence. Put more transformation in world knowledge, capability, relationships, or environment than in fixed internal psychology.

### Hybrid
Common in RPGs. Some identity/voice/arc is authored while other dimensions are player-defined. Hybrids require especially clear boundaries to avoid expectation mismatch.

## Character arc types
Use labels descriptively, not mechanically:
- **Positive transformation:** abandons insufficient belief/behavior and integrates a stronger truth.
- **Flat/steadfast arc:** character already holds a needed truth and changes the world/others under pressure.
- **Negative/corruption arc:** pressure deepens a false belief or compromises values.
- **Disillusionment arc:** false ideal collapses; outcome may be painful but clarifying.
- **Recovery/redemption:** accountability + changed behavior + cost; forgiveness is not guaranteed.
- **Tragic failure:** character recognizes or nearly recognizes the needed change but cannot integrate it.

## Character pressure test
For each major turning point ask:
1. What does the character want right now?
2. What value/relationship blocks the easiest path?
3. What are they afraid the event means about them?
4. What choice do they make?
5. What does that choice reveal?
6. What consequence makes the next choice harder?

If the plot could happen identically with a different personality, the character is not driving enough of the story.

## Relationship arcs
Relationships need their own dramatic state, not just two independent character arcs.
Track:
- starting assumption each holds about the other;
- attraction/utility/dependency;
- sources of friction;
- trust;
- power/status balance;
- unspoken need;
- rupture point;
- revelation/reinterpretation;
- renegotiation;
- final relationship state.

Useful relationship state axes may include trust, respect, intimacy, fear, obligation, ideological alignment, and dependency. Do not turn every relationship into a visible number; the axes are for authoring clarity.

## Companions and repeated interaction
Ask:
- what repeated gameplay context lets this character reveal themselves?
- what new behavior becomes available as the relationship changes?
- how does the companion respond to world/quest decisions?
- which callbacks make the player feel remembered?
- what is the consequence of neglect, betrayal, or disagreement?

## Antagonists and antagonistic forces
A strong antagonist does not need moral equivalence, but does need coherent pressure.
Define:
- goal;
- value system;
- why their method makes sense to them;
- what they correctly perceive about the protagonist/world;
- what line they cross or refuse to cross;
- how their strategy adapts;
- why simply walking away is difficult.

An institution, environment, social order, curse, war, or systemic condition can be the antagonistic force; give it understandable rules and escalating pressure.

## Game-specific character validation
- **Voice blind test:** remove names from 10 representative lines; can a reader identify speakers?
- **Pressure consistency:** do decisions follow established values unless change is motivated?
- **Agency consistency:** does the authored character override player-selected identity/morality?
- **Relationship memory:** do important choices receive later acknowledgment?
- **Arc evidence:** can each claimed change be tied to decisions/actions, not just final exposition?
- **Attachment test:** what does the player repeatedly do with/for/against this character that creates attachment?

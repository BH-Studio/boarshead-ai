# Scenes, Dialogue, Quests, and Environmental Storytelling

## Scene architecture
A scene is a unit of change, not merely a location where dialogue happens.
Default dramatic model:

`OBJECTIVE → OBSTACLE → TACTIC / PLAYER CHOICE → TURN → CONSEQUENCE → EXIT HOOK`

At least one meaningful state should change:
- knowledge;
- relationship;
- commitment;
- risk;
- plan;
- location/access;
- resource;
- identity/status;
- world state.

If nothing changes, the scene may be atmosphere, characterization, or setup; that can be valid, but recognize the function.

## Scene card
For substantial scenes record:
- scene purpose;
- POV/player role;
- location/time;
- entry state;
- participant objectives;
- hidden information;
- conflict/obstacle;
- choice points;
- turn/reversal;
- mandatory information;
- optional information;
- emotional target;
- exit state;
- callbacks/setup/payoff;
- branch/state dependencies.

## Dialogue craft
Dialogue should perform character action.
Track each major speaker's:
- objective;
- tactic;
- subtext;
- status/power;
- shared history;
- information advantage/disadvantage;
- emotional concealment;
- vocabulary and syntax;
- metaphor/reference habits;
- humor style;
- conflict style;
- forbidden topics;
- pressure behavior.

### Exposition budget
Before writing exposition ask:
- Does the player need this now?
- Can environment/action/quest behavior show it?
- Who has a reason to say it?
- Why does the listener not already know it?
- What emotional or tactical objective makes the information part of conflict?

### Subtext test
For an important line ask: what does the speaker *want the listener to do/believe/feel*, and what are they refusing to say directly?

### Blind voice test
Remove speaker labels from representative lines. If every line could belong to any character, voice needs work.

## Dialogue choices
Dialogue options may serve different purposes:
- express identity/attitude;
- ask for information;
- negotiate;
- threaten/charm/deceive;
- change relationship;
- commit to action;
- choose values.

The displayed option should not misrepresent the spoken line or hidden intensity. If a choice is irreversible or unusually consequential, give the player adequate contextual signal unless surprise is itself the intended design.

## Barks and reactive lines
Barks need event, priority, cooldown, repeat policy, interruption rules, context, and fallback considerations. Narrative value comes from acknowledging player/world state without overwhelming the audio/text channel.

## Quest narrative
Use Story Circle frequently for quest-scale arcs:
comfort/current state → need/problem → enter unfamiliar situation → adapt/investigate → reach apparent goal → pay/complicate → return/reframe → changed state.

Quest specification:
- player-facing premise;
- narrative purpose;
- player goal;
- dramatic question;
- stake;
- involved characters/factions;
- discovery/evidence;
- escalation;
- optional approaches;
- major choice(s);
- climax/decision;
- aftermath;
- world/relationship/state changes;
- rewards with narrative meaning;
- re-entry to larger arcs;
- failure/abandon/revisit behavior.

Avoid quests whose story is "NPC gives task → player does unrelated gameplay → NPC thanks player." Make the gameplay action change or reveal the narrative problem.

## Environmental storytelling
Treat spaces as evidence.
Design:
- focal story question;
- 3–7 evidence pieces of varying explicitness;
- spatial ordering/visibility;
- player activity that reveals evidence;
- contradiction or ambiguity;
- character/world connection;
- reinforcement elsewhere;
- optional depth for explorers.

Avoid repeating the exact same fact in prop, audio log, dialogue, and codex unless redundancy is intentionally serving accessibility or emphasis.

## Narrative-level design bridge
GDC level-design guidance emphasizes that story setting and pace can shape spaces and asset placement. When environment is narrative-critical, produce a narrative intent brief for level design rather than prescribing geometry.
Source: GDC Vault, Jolie Menzel, "A Narrative Approach to Level Design": https://gdcvault.com/play/1024302/Level-Design-Workshop-A-Narrative

## Validation checklist
- Does every key scene change something?
- Can each important character's dialogue be identified by voice?
- Is required information motivated?
- Are dialogue choices semantically honest?
- Does each quest have a dramatic question beyond completion?
- Do optional quests reinforce character/world/theme?
- Can environmental evidence be understood without a lore dump?
- Are callbacks and setup/payoff tracked?

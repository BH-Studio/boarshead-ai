# Worldbuilding, Lore, and Speculative Systems

## Worldbuilding goal
Build a world that **generates stories, characters, conflicts, and gameplay**, not a reference encyclopedia that merely explains itself.

Pixar's public storytelling curriculum summarizes a useful foundational idea: story emerges when **world and character meet**. In games, add a third term: **player action**.

`WORLD PRESSURE × CHARACTER VALUES × PLAYER ACTION = STORY POSSIBILITY`

## Causal Worldbuilding Chain
Use this as the default worldbuilding engine:

`FOUNDATIONAL PREMISE`
→ `RULES / MATERIAL CONDITIONS`
→ `LIMITATIONS / COSTS / SCARCITIES`
→ `WHO CONTROLS RESOURCES OR KNOWLEDGE`
→ `INSTITUTIONS / CUSTOMS / TABOOS`
→ `EVERYDAY LIFE`
→ `CONFLICTS / INEQUALITIES / OPPORTUNITIES`
→ `CHARACTER IDENTITIES & RELATIONSHIPS`
→ `QUEST / STORY / GAMEPLAY HOOKS`

If a lore element cannot connect to this chain, it may be decorative. Decorative lore is allowed, but label it honestly and keep its cost proportional to value.

## World layers
Use only the layers relevant to the project:
- cosmology/metaphysics;
- geography, climate, astronomy;
- ecology, food, disease, resources;
- deep history and recent memory;
- cultures/peoples and internal diversity;
- family/kinship/gender roles where relevant;
- politics, law, legitimacy, bureaucracy;
- economy, class, labor, trade;
- religion, philosophy, ritual, taboo;
- education, knowledge, media, propaganda;
- technology, infrastructure, magic;
- military/security/crime;
- language, naming, slang;
- art, entertainment, cuisine, dress;
- daily routines and material culture;
- factions, guilds, corporations, movements;
- borders, migration, travel, communication;
- myths, rumors, false histories, mysteries.

## Network worldbuilding
Large worlds become more manageable when designed as **relationships**, not just pages of facts.
For each key node (faction, location, person, institution, resource, event), record:
- what it depends on;
- what depends on it;
- allies;
- rivals;
- contested resources;
- historical links;
- information flows;
- player-facing entry points.

This makes a world easier to reveal incrementally and exposes isolated lore that never affects anything.

Industry source: GDC Vault, Gerben Grave, "Connected Worlds: Building Dynamic Storyworlds Using Network Theory": https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1027045/Game-Narrative-Summit-Connected-Worlds

## Depth before density
For major world premises, prefer exploring ramifications before adding unrelated concepts.
Example questions:
- If resurrection exists, what changes about inheritance, murder law, religion, war, insurance, and grief?
- If interstellar travel takes months, what does that do to families, politics, supply chains, and information?
- If one resource enables power, who controls extraction, training, and black markets?

## Sanderson's Laws as optional speculative-system craft lenses
These are not universal laws; Sanderson himself presents them as guidelines that work for his writing.

### First Law — understandability and conflict resolution
The more a supernatural/speculative system is used to solve important conflict, the more the audience needs enough understanding for the solution to feel earned rather than arbitrary.
Use soft/unknown systems primarily for wonder, danger, mystery, or problems unless the audience has been prepared for solution rules.

Source: https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/sandersons-first-law

### Second Law — limitations > powers
Costs, limits, weaknesses, access restrictions, social consequences, and tradeoffs create more dramatic pressure than a list of capabilities.
Ask: what does using this power make harder?

Source: https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/sandersons-second-law

### Third Law — expand before adding
Deepen interactions among established ideas before adding new powers, factions, species, or rules. This improves coherence and reduces lore sprawl.

Source: https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/sandersons-third-law-of-magic

## Worldbuilding for games
Every major world fact should be tested against at least one player-facing channel:
- environment/level design;
- NPC behavior;
- faction behavior;
- economy/resources;
- quest conflict;
- dialogue;
- visual language;
- audio/music;
- mechanics/rules;
- collectible/environmental evidence;
- world-state change.

If the only delivery method is a codex entry, ask whether the fact is worth authoring.

## Cultural and historical inspiration
Distinguish:
- **inspired by** — transformed creative influence;
- **analogous to** — designed comparison;
- **representing** — claims a real people/time/place;
- **fantasy composite** — combines elements from multiple sources.

The closer the work claims real representation, the stronger the factual/authenticity burden. Avoid treating a culture as a monolithic costume set. Include internal class, regional, generational, ideological, and personal variation where relevant.

## World mystery
Do not explain everything. Track separately:
- author truth;
- public belief;
- faction belief;
- character belief;
- player-known facts;
- false rumor;
- unresolved mystery.

Mystery is coherent when the author knows enough rules to prevent accidental contradiction even if the player never receives full explanation.

## Worldbuilding validation
- Can five major lore elements be connected by causality rather than coincidence?
- Does each major faction have material interests plus values?
- What does an ordinary person's day look like?
- What is scarce?
- What is dangerous?
- What behavior is normal here but strange to the player?
- Which historical event still changes current choices?
- What would collapse if one key resource/institution disappeared?
- Can the world support many stories beyond the main plot?

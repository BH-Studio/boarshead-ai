# Interactive Narrative, Player Agency, and Branching Structures

## Core principle
Interactive narrative does not begin with "How many endings?" It begins with:
**What part of the story does the player author?**

Possible authorship domains:
- action/strategy;
- attitude/voice;
- identity/backstory;
- relationships;
- values/morality;
- information/discovery order;
- quest priorities;
- plot events;
- world state;
- ending.

A game can offer strong agency in one domain and little in another. Make the boundaries legible.

## Choice function taxonomy
### Expressive choice
Lets the player state attitude, identity, humor, emotion, or roleplay. It may not change plot. Acknowledge it socially or in voice when feasible.

### Strategic choice
Changes resources, risk, route, tactics, or future options.

### Relational choice
Changes trust, intimacy, obligation, loyalty, fear, rivalry, or availability.

### Epistemic choice
Changes what the player learns, when they learn it, or which interpretation they build.

### Plot/world choice
Changes events, who lives, faction control, mission availability, ending, or durable world state.

Do not pretend all choices are plot choices. Expressive choices are valuable when presented honestly.

## Consequence horizon
For meaningful choices map:
1. **intention** — what the player thinks they are choosing;
2. **immediate response** — visible local reaction;
3. **state change** — what is now different;
4. **delayed consequence** — later effect;
5. **cumulative consequence** — thresholds/patterns across many choices;
6. **acknowledgment** — evidence that the game remembered;
7. **payoff** — final narrative meaning.

Player trust depends heavily on acknowledgment, not only branch size.

## Sam Kabo Ashwell's recurring choice structures
Ashwell describes common structural patterns that are useful as a vocabulary rather than rigid categories.

### Time Cave
Heavy divergence, little rejoining, many endings. Strong freedom/replay; high content cost and low content coverage.

### Gauntlet
Mostly linear spine with dead ends, failures, or quick rejoining. Easy to author and preserves major story control; can feel constrained.

### Branch-and-Bottleneck
Choices diverge, then rejoin around shared major events. State tracking preserves the effects of prior choices. Excellent for manageable authored main stories with personalized context.

### Quest
Modular clusters organized around journey/geography with multiple local approaches and relatively few final outcomes. Strong for RPG/adventure exploration.

### Open Map
Player can revisit major nodes in a persistent geography. Strong exploration; weaker automatic pacing and higher continuity/state burden.

### Sorting Hat
Early decisions accumulate to assign the player to a major route. Gives strong differentiation but may require effectively multiple content tracks.

### Floating Modules
Narrative pieces become available based on state rather than a central tree. Flexible/systemic, but hard to author and test without strong state rules.

### Loop and Grow / Hub-Spoke
Return to repeated hub/cycle while new options unlock and old ones close. Good for routine, relationship growth, time loops, seasons, repeated expeditions.

Primary source: Sam Kabo Ashwell, "Standard Patterns in Choice-Based Games": https://heterogenoustasks.wordpress.com/2015/01/26/standard-patterns-in-choice-based-games/

## Branch selection criteria
Choose structure based on:
- desired player control;
- replay expectations;
- fixed story anchors;
- amount of state tracking available;
- authoring budget;
- required emotional pacing;
- open-world ordering;
- number of writers;
- localization/VO cost;
- continuity burden.

## Multiple middles vs multiple endings
A game can provide meaningful agency through different **routes, relationships, discoveries, and middle states** while converging to a smaller number of endings. Ending count is a poor proxy for agency.

Industry example: GDC, Hannah Nicklin, "Multiple Middles & Mutazione": https://gdcvault.com/play/1026911/Multiple-Middles

## Choice wording and expectation
The choice UI is part of narrative contract.
Audit:
- Does the label match what the protagonist actually says/does?
- Does the player understand whether the choice is emotional, tactical, or irreversible?
- Are hidden consequences thematically fair even when not predictable?
- Is "no choice" sometimes stronger than fake choice?

## Player-character agency conflict
A common narrative failure occurs when the player selects one identity/value and the authored protagonist later behaves as though a different identity was selected.
For every authored "must happen" moment ask:
- can all supported player identities plausibly do this?
- if not, should the choice space be narrower earlier?
- should the protagonist be more authored?
- should the event be external rather than a forced internal decision?

## Interactive narrative validation
- branch/state matrix;
- consequence-horizon audit;
- rejoin acknowledgment check;
- open-order permutation test;
- ending traceability to prior state;
- dead-content/content-coverage estimate;
- replay-value hypothesis;
- choice-label vs actual-action check;
- player interviews: "what did you think that choice would do?"
